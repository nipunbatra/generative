Products of many small numbers are awkward

Surprise $= -\log p$, and why the log

What should "surprise" do?

1 · a certain event: no surprise. $-\log 1 = 0$.

2 · rarer means more surprising. $-\log p$ grows as $p$ shrinks.

3 · two independent digits: probabilities multiply, surprises should add. Only the log turns products into sums.

So the betting-game product becomes a sum of surprises, one per digit. Natural log, so the unit is the nat.

Following Artem Kirsanov's argument: the additivity requirement forces the logarithm. The base only changes the unit (bits for log base 2).

Average surprise, when you believe the truth

Entropy: $H(P) = 0.500$ nats per digit

$$H(P) = \sum_i P_i\,(-\log P_i) = \underbrace{0.8}_{\text{how often a 1}}\times 0.223 + \underbrace{0.2}_{\text{how often a 7}}\times 1.609 = 0.500$$

The weights are how often each digit really appears: eight in ten, two in ten. The batch of ten digits from the game, averaged.

Even the perfect model is surprised by 0.500 nats per digit on average: the data are random.

Same data, a different scorer

Cross-entropy: $H(P, Q) = 0.592$ nats per digit

$$H(P, Q) = \sum_i \cx{P_i}\,(-\log \cdec{Q_i}) = 0.8 \times 0.511 + 0.2 \times 0.916 = 0.592$$

Still weighted by $P$

the digits still come from the real data: eight 1s, two 7s.

Scored by $Q$

each digit's surprise now uses the model's probability.

Believing the wrong model costs more surprise on the same data: 0.592 instead of 0.500.

Kirsanov's rigged coin: you only get unusually surprised by ten heads in a row because you wrongly believe the coin is fair. The data did not change; your model did.

The extra surprise

Notation we will use all lecture

$\E$ means "average over"

$$\E_{x\sim P}\big[f(x)\big] = \sum_x P(x)\,f(x)$$
exact, for a sum over outcomes
$$\E_{z\sim q}\big[f(z)\big] = \int q(z)\,f(z)\,dz$$
exact, for a continuous variable
$$\E_{x\sim P}\big[f(x)\big] \approx \frac1S\sum_{s=1}^{S} f(x_s),\quad x_s\sim P$$
estimated from samples
KL is an average: $\KL(P\|Q) = \E_{x\sim P}\big[\log\tfrac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\big]$. Five draws 1, 1, 7, 1, 1 give $\tfrac15(4\times 0.288 - 0.693) = 0.092$.

The subscript says which distribution the average is taken under: x ∼ P means "x is drawn from P". This matters because KL changes when you swap it.

Those five draws happen to be exactly 80/20, so the estimate matches. With random draws it would fluctuate around 0.092.

Why KL can never be negative

One picture: $\log r$ lies under its tangent

Let $r = Q_i / P_i$. The curve $\log r$ never rises above the line $r - 1$.

$$\KL = \sum_i P_i\,(-\log r_i) \;\ge\; \sum_i P_i\,(1 - r_i)$$
$$= \sum_i P_i - \sum_i Q_i = 1 - 1 = 0$$

Equality only when every $r_i = 1$: $Q = P$. No model beats the truth on the truth's own data.

The tangent picture is from Jia-Bin Huang's KL video. Red segments: our two digits, r = 0.75 for a 1 and r = 2 for a 7. The gaps between the line and the curve are what makes KL positive.

What the number means

KL is how fast evidence piles up

$$\frac{p(\text{data}\mid P)}{p(\text{data}\mid Q)} \approx e^{\,n\,\KL(P\|Q)}$$

After 10 digits the truth is 2.5× more likely; after 50, 97×; after 100, 9,400×.

A small KL means the two models are hard to tell apart from data; a large one means a few samples settle it.

Not a distance

Swap the arguments, get a different number

$$\KL(Q\|P) = 0.6\log\frac{0.6}{0.8} + 0.4\log\frac{0.4}{0.2}$$
$$= -0.1726 + 0.2773 = 0.1046$$

$0.105 \neq 0.092$: KL is not symmetric. "How wrong is $Q$ about $P$" differs from "how wrong is $P$ about $Q$".

Push $q \to 0$ and $\KL(P\|Q) \to \infty$. Never give zero probability to something that happens.

Why training code says "cross-entropy"

Cross-entropy = entropy + KL

$H(P,Q)$$=$$\displaystyle -\sum_i P_i\log Q_i$ cross-entropy: the loss you train classifiers with $=$$\displaystyle -\sum_i P_i\log P_i \;+\; \sum_i P_i\big(\log P_i - \log Q_i\big)$ add and subtract $\sum_i P_i\log P_i$ $=$$\displaystyle \underbrace{H(P)}_{\text{entropy: fixed by the data}} \;+\; \KL(P\,\|\,Q)$

Lowering cross-entropy can only lower the KL part. So does maximum likelihood: it minimises $\KL(\text{data}\,\|\,\text{model})$.

Together: H(P, Q) = 0.592 nats. Remember the direction: data first. That direction will explain why VAEs blur.

The version the VAE needs

KL between two bumps: an area

For continuous variables the sum becomes an integral:

$$\KL(q\|p) = \int q(z)\,\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z)}\,dz$$

Take a toy cloud $q = \N(1, 0.5^2)$ and $p = \N(0,1)$, the prior. (Our 3's real $q$ is far narrower: Part 7.)

The shaded area, computed numerically, is 0.818 nats.

Can we get 0.818 without a computer? Yes: for two Gaussians the integral has a formula. Two steps.

Numerical integral of the lower curve: 0.8181. The grey part is where q is below p and contributes negatively, like the 7 row earlier.

Gaussian KL, step 1 of 2

Write out the log of the ratio

$\log q(z)$$=$$\displaystyle -\log\sigma - \tfrac12\log 2\pi - \frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$ log of $\N(z;\mu,\sigma^2)$ $\log p(z)$$=$$\displaystyle -\tfrac12\log 2\pi - \frac{z^2}{2}$ log of $\N(z;0,1)$ $\log\dfrac{q(z)}{p(z)}$$=$$\displaystyle -\log\sigma - \frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{z^2}{2}$ subtract: the $\tfrac12\log 2\pi$ terms cancel

Three terms. Next: average each one over $z \sim q$.

Gaussian KL, step 2 of 2

Average each term over the cloud $q$

$\E_q\big[(z-\mu)^2\big] = \sigma^2$ (that is the variance)
$\E_q\big[z^2\big] = \mu^2 + \sigma^2$ (mean squared + variance)
$$\KL = -\log\sigma - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{\mu^2+\sigma^2}{2} = \frac12\Big(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2\Big)$$
$$\mu = 1,\ \sigma = 0.5:\quad \tfrac12\big(1 + 0.25 - 1 + 1.386\big) = 0.818 \ \checkmark$$

Per coordinate: moving the mean costs $\tfrac12\mu^2$; a width other than 1 costs $\tfrac12(\sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2)$, zero only at $\sigma = 1$. Several coordinates: add one term each.

The check matches the shaded area from two slides ago. This exact formula is what the VAE code computes.

The goal, written down

Find the $q$ with the smallest KL to the posterior

$$q^{\star} = \arg\min_{q\,\in\,\mathcal Q}\; \KL\big(\,q(z)\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\,\big)$$

$\mathcal Q$: the shapes we allow

Diagonal Gaussians. For our 2-number code: 4 numbers, a centre $m$ and two widths $s$.

Why $q$ first?

KL averages under its first argument. $q$ is ours, so we can draw from it. The other order would need draws from the posterior: the thing we cannot get.

The catch

$p_\theta(z\mid x) = p_\theta(x,z)\,/\,p_\theta(x)$, and $p_\theta(x)$ is the integral we cannot do. We cannot even evaluate this KL.

Four short moves get $p_\theta(x)$ out of the way.

This is KL(q ‖ p), the "reverse" or mode-seeking direction from the end of Part 4. Here the choice is forced by practicality: we can only average under a distribution we can sample.

From the KL to the ELBO · move 1 of 4

Write out the KL

$$\KL\big(q(z)\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\big)$$
$$= \E_{z\sim q}\big[\,\log q(z) - \hl{\log p_\theta(z\mid x)}\,\big]$$
The definition from Part 4: the average, under the first argument, of the log of the ratio. The highlighted term is the one we cannot compute.

From the KL to the ELBO · move 2 of 4

Bayes: the posterior is the joint over the evidence

$$\E_{q}\big[\log q(z) - \log p_\theta(z\mid x)\big]$$
$$= \E_{q}\big[\log q(z) - \log p_\theta(x,z)\big] \;+\; \hl{\log p_\theta(x)}$$
$\log p_\theta(z\mid x) = \log p_\theta(x,z) - \log p_\theta(x)$. The evidence has no $z$ in it: the same number for every draw, so it comes out of the average.

The joint $p_\theta(x,z) = p(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)$ is easy: prior times decoder. All the difficulty is now in one number.

From the KL to the ELBO · move 3 of 4

Move the evidence to the other side

$$\KL\big(q\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\big) = \E_{q}\big[\log q(z) - \log p_\theta(x,z)\big] + \log p_\theta(x)$$
$$\log p_\theta(x) = \underbrace{\hl{\E_{q}\big[\log p_\theta(x,z) - \log q(z)\big]}}_{\ELBO(q)} \;+\; \KL\big(q\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\big)$$
Rearrange, and give the highlighted average a name: the ELBO. Every piece of it is computable: the joint is prior times decoder, and $q$ is ours.

Why "Evidence Lower BOund"? The last move.

From the KL to the ELBO · move 4 of 4

The KL is never negative, so the ELBO is a lower bound

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \ELBO(q) + \underbrace{\KL\big(q\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\big)}_{\ge\, 0} \;\ge\; \ELBO(q)$$

Our 3, two choices of $q$. The right end, $\log p_\theta(x)$, is fixed by the model; it does not move when $q$ changes.

At q = prior the KL to the prior is 0, so the ELBO there is just the average log p(x | z) of random codes: the blind sampling of Part 3. The next slides find the second q.

Unpacking the ELBO · step 1 of 3

The joint is prior times decoder

$$\ELBO(q) = \E_{q}\big[\log p_\theta(x,z) - \log q(z)\big]$$
$$= \E_{q}\Big[\hl{\log p_\theta(x\mid z) + \log p(z) - \log q(z)}\Big]$$
$p_\theta(x,z) = p(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)$: the generative story from Part 3. The log of a product is a sum of logs.

Why unpack? To see which parts are the decoder and which are the prior, and to get two terms with names.

Unpacking the ELBO · step 2 of 3

The average of a sum is the sum of the averages

$$\E_{q}\Big[\log p_\theta(x\mid z) + \log p(z) - \log q(z)\Big]$$
$$= \hl{\E_{q}\big[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)\big]} \;+\; \hl{\E_{q}\big[\log p(z) - \log q(z)\big]}$$
$\E[a + b] = \E[a] + \E[b]$. Group the decoder term on its own, and the two terms that only involve $z$ together.

Unpacking the ELBO · step 3 of 3

Recognise the second average: a KL to the prior

$$\E_{q}\big[\log p(z) - \log q(z)\big]$$
$$= -\,\E_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z)}\right] = \hl{-\KL\big(q(z)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}$$
Flip the sign to flip the fraction. Then use the definition: $\KL(q\|p) = \E_{z\sim q}\big[\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z)}\big]$, the average extra surprise.

And for Gaussians we already have its formula: $\tfrac12\sum_j(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-1-\log\sigma_j^2)$.

Put the pieces together

The ELBO, in symbols and in words

$$\ELBO = \underbrace{\E_{z\sim\cenc q}\big[\cdec{\log p_\theta(x\mid z)}\big]}_{\text{rebuild } x \text{ from codes in its cloud}} \;-\; \underbrace{\closs{\KL\big(q(z)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}}_{\text{keep the cloud near } \N(0,I)}$$
First term: the autoencoder's reconstruction score, with the code drawn from the cloud.
Second term: the Gaussian KL from Part 4, in closed form.

The two wishes from the end of Part 2, derived rather than invented: rebuild well, and keep codes where the prior can find them.

Variational inference by hand, for our 3

Four numbers, gradient ascent on the ELBO

x = our_3                                  # 784 pixels, fixed
m     = torch.zeros(2, requires_grad=True)   # q's centre
log_s = torch.zeros(2, requires_grad=True)   # q's log-widths
opt = torch.optim.Adam([m, log_s], lr=0.03)

for step in range(800):
    z = m + log_s.exp() * torch.randn(64, 2)    # 64 draws from q
    rec = -bce(decoder(z), x).sum(1).mean()    # E_q[log p(x|z)]
    kl  = 0.5 * (m**2 + (2*log_s).exp() - 1 - 2*log_s).sum()
    loss = -(rec - kl)                           # minus the ELBO
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

A bonus: with $q$, even $p_\theta(x)$ becomes easy to estimate

Sample codes where the patch is, then reweight

$$p_\theta(x) = \int q(z)\,\frac{p(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)}{q(z)}\,dz = \E_{z\sim q}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{q(z)}\right] \approx \frac1S\sum_{s=1}^{S}\frac{p_\theta(x, z_s)}{q(z_s)}$$

20 runs of 1,000 draws each. From the prior, the estimates scatter from −134.8 to −127.6. From $q$: −128.74, give or take 0.017.

This is importance sampling: multiply and divide by q, then average under q. With 2 numbers per code, blind sampling from the prior is only noisy; with 16 it would almost never land in the patch (Part 3).

Put a log in front of this average and push it inside the average: that is the Jensen route to the ELBO in the appendix.

Amortised inference: learn to predict $q$

Our 3: ELBO −128.83 by hand in 800 steps, −129.3 from the encoder in one pass. Pay once during training; a little worse, thousands of times cheaper.

The encoder outputs q_φ(z | x) = N(μ_φ(x), diag σ_φ(x)²). The small loss in ELBO is called the amortisation gap. Both numbers: the ELBO of our 3 under each q, 20,000 draws.

Over the first 20 test digits the median gap is 1.38 nats. Per-image VI from N(0, I) is itself a local search: on one digit it ended −24.6 nats relative to the encoder (stuck), on another 85.18 (the encoder was far off). Real comparisons use the encoder to start VI.

Put the pieces together

Encoder + decoder + ELBO = the variational autoencoder

"Variational"

"Autoencoder"

image → encoder → code → decoder → image: the shape from Part 2, now with a cloud for a code.

Trained together

Pushing on $\theta$ raises the ELBO mostly by making the data more probable: a better model. Pushing on $\varphi$ shrinks the gap: a better $q$.

VI by hand closed the gap to 0.08 nats in 800 steps; the encoder's single pass leaves 0.55. That small loss is the price of amortising.

ELBO under the encoder's q for our 3: 20,000 draws. log p(x): a fine grid around the patch (spacing 0.0016; the 901 × 901 grid of Part 3 gives −128.7, slightly coarser).

  2. Rearranged: $\log p_\theta(x) = \ELBO + \KL$. Maximising the ELBO minimises the KL.

Part 6 of 7

Backpropagating through a random draw

VI by hand wrote each draw as $m + s\cdot\eps$ without comment. The VAE loss also uses a $z$ drawn from the encoder's cloud, and to train the encoder, gradients must get from the loss back to $\mu$ and $\sigma$ through that draw. Here is why the way we write the draw is the whole trick.

The problem

A sampling step sits in the middle of the graph

In PyTorch, Normal(mu, sigma).sample() returns a tensor with no gradient history: the graph is cut at z.

We want the gradient of the expected loss with respect to μ and σ: if the cloud moves a little, how does the average loss change? There are other estimators (score function / REINFORCE), but they are noisy. Here is a simple trick that works for Gaussians.

The trick

Draw standard noise first, then stretch and shift it

$$z = \mu + \sigma\,\eps,\quad \eps\sim\N(0,1) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad z \sim \N(\mu,\sigma^2)$$

Same distribution as before (the $\mu = 1$, $\sigma = 0.5$ cloud of the Gaussian KL slide). But the randomness now enters as an input, $\eps$, that does not depend on any weights.

Follow one dot (red): ε = −0.4, scaled by σ = 0.5 to −0.2, shifted by μ = 1 to 0.8. Each z is a deterministic function of (μ, σ, ε).

If μ increases a little, every dot moves right by the same amount. If σ increases, each dot moves away from the centre in proportion to its ε. That is exactly the derivative.

The fix

Now the path from the loss to $\mu$ and $\sigma$ is ordinary arithmetic

Formally: ∇ E_{z∼q}[f(z)] = E_{ε∼𝒩(0,1)}[∇ f(μ + σε)], under mild regularity conditions. We check this numerically a few slides from now.

Chain rule, symbol by symbol

Take $f(z) = z^2$ as the loss

$$z = \mu + \sigma\eps \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial \mu} = 1, \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial \sigma} = \eps, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} = 2z$$
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial \mu} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\cdot\frac{\partial z}{\partial \mu} = 2z\cdot 1$$
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial \sigma} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\cdot\frac{\partial z}{\partial \sigma} = 2z\cdot\eps$$

The noise value $\eps$ appears in the $\sigma$-gradient: a draw far from the centre pushes $\sigma$ harder.

ε is treated exactly like an input pixel: a fixed number for this forward and backward pass. A new ε is drawn next step.

Now plug in numbers

$\mu = 1,\ \sigma = 0.5,\ \eps = -0.4$

Finite-difference check with ε held fixed: μ → 1.001 gives z = 0.801 and f = 0.641601, slope 1.601 ≈ 1.6. If you resample ε between the two evaluations, the check becomes noise.

One more link in the chain

The network outputs $v = \log\sigma^2$, not $\sigma$

$\sigma$$=$$e^{v/2}$ $v$ can be any real number; $\sigma$ is always positive $\dfrac{\partial\sigma}{\partial v}$$=$$\tfrac12 e^{v/2} = \dfrac{\sigma}{2} = 0.25$ derivative of $e^{v/2}$ $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial v}$$=$$\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial \sigma}\cdot\dfrac{\partial\sigma}{\partial v} = -0.64 \times 0.25 = -0.16$ one more chain-rule factor

Autograd does exactly this. The point of doing it by hand once: no step is magic.

Is one draw enough?

One draw is noisy; the average is exactly right

$$\E_\eps\big[f\big] = \E\big[(\mu+\sigma\eps)^2\big] = \mu^2 + \sigma^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\E[f] = 2\mu = 2, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}\E[f] = 2\sigma = 1$$

Our single $\eps$ gave 1.6 and $-0.64$. Averaged over 10000 draws: 2.01 and 1.02. SGD only needs the right answer on average.

Each histogram: the per-sample gradient for 10,000 values of ε. The red dashed line is our ε = −0.4. The solid line, the average, sits on the exact gradient of the expected loss. Noisy but unbiased: exactly what stochastic gradient descent needs.

In PyTorch

Three lines

# encoder outputs, shape [B, 2] each
mu, logvar = encoder(x)
std = torch.exp(0.5 * logvar)     # sigma = exp(v / 2)
eps = torch.randn_like(std)       # standard normal, no grad
z   = mu + std * eps              # differentiable in mu, std

Why output $\log\sigma^2$? It can be any real number; $\sigma$ must be positive.

Normal(mu, std).rsample() does the same thing; .sample() drops the gradient.

For our 3: $\mu = (0.3,\, −1.93)$, $\eps = (0.62,\, −1.15)$ gives $z = (0.33,\, −1.99)$.

Common bug: using exp(logvar) where the standard deviation is needed, which squares σ. Another: calling .sample() and wondering why the encoder never learns.

End of Part 6

What we did, and what comes next

What we did

  1. Sampling $z$ directly cuts the gradient path to $\mu$ and $\sigma$.
  2. Write $z = \mu + \sigma\eps$ with $\eps \sim \N(0, 1)$: same distribution, randomness as an input.
  3. $\partial z/\partial\mu = 1$, $\partial z/\partial\sigma = \eps$; one draw is noisy, the average is exact.
  4. In PyTorch: three lines, or rsample().

Most of the cost is $-\log\sigma^2$: the price of being precise. Reconstruction wants precision; the KL resists.

If σ were 1 and μ 0, the KL would be 0, but then the code would carry no information about which digit this is, and reconstruction would be poor. The tension between the two terms shapes the code space.

One training step

The whole algorithm fits on a slide

mu, logvar = model.encoder(x)              # [B,2], [B,2]
z = mu + torch.exp(0.5*logvar) * torch.randn_like(mu)
logits = model.decoder(z)                  # [B,784]
recon = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(
          logits, x.flatten(1), reduction="none").sum(1)
kl = 0.5 * (mu**2 + logvar.exp() - 1 - logvar).sum(1)
loss = (recon + kl).mean()                 # per image, then batch
opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

Encode, reparameterise, decode.

Sum the BCE over all 784 pixels of each image.

Sum the KL over the 2 code coordinates of each image.

Add per image, average over the batch, step both encoder and decoder.

Averaging BCE over pixels instead of summing silently makes the KL 784× stronger.

An honest look

Why are VAE samples blurry?

1. It fits $\KL(\text{data}\,\|\,\text{model})$. Like the wide Gaussian on our bumps, it must cover every training digit, so when unsure it spreads probability.

2. Pixels are independent given $z$. The decoder outputs one probability per pixel. If a stroke could be here or one pixel over, the best $\pi$ is grey in both places.

3. We show $\pi$, the mean, not a sample. A sample would be speckled instead of grey.

$\pi$ (the mean)

a sample

Not a law of nature: bigger codes, better decoders and better likelihoods all sharpen VAEs.

Three ways to generate

Same goal, three different training signals

Autoregressive: exact likelihood, product of next-token probabilities; slow sampling. VAE: explicit latent model, trained on a lower bound; tends to blur. GAN: no likelihood at all, trained against a learned judge; sharp, can miss modes and is finicky to train.

None is "the best". Modern image models (after the GAN lecture) took a fourth route.

Before you leave

Three exit questions

  1. To generate a new digit with a trained VAE, which parts of the network do you use? The prior and the decoder only: $z\sim\N(0, I)$, then decode. The encoder is for training.
  2. Two different KL divergences appear in the VAE story. What does each compare, and which one is in the loss? $\KL(q\,\|\,\text{prior})$ is in the loss (6.97 for our 3). $\KL(q\,\|\,\text{true posterior})$ is the gap between the ELBO and $\log p_\theta(x)$ (0.55).
  3. We never compute $p_\theta(x)$. Why does maximising the ELBO still pull $q$ towards the true posterior? $\log p_\theta(x) = \ELBO + \KL(q\,\|\,\text{posterior})$, and the left side does not depend on $q$. Whatever the ELBO gains, the KL loses.

Appendix

A second route to the ELBO

Part 5 reached the ELBO from $\KL(q\,\|\,\text{posterior})$. Many textbooks reach it from $\log p_\theta(x)$ with Jensen's inequality instead. Same bound, one more tool.

Appendix · the ELBO through Jensen · move 1 of 4

Start from what we want

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \log \int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz$$
Maximum likelihood wants this number large for our 3, and for every image in the data. The integral over all codes is the part we cannot compute.

A second route to the bound of Part 5, starting from $\log p_\theta(x)$ instead of the KL.

Recall p(x, z) = p(z) p(x | z): prior times decoder. Each of the next slides makes exactly one change. Ask the class to name the move before you click.

Appendix · the ELBO through Jensen · move 2 of 4

Multiply and divide by the encoder's cloud

$$\log \int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz$$
$$= \log \int \hl{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\;\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{\hl{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}}\,dz$$
Multiplying and dividing by the same positive number changes nothing. We are free to choose any $q$; we choose the encoder's Gaussian, which is positive everywhere.

Why bother? Because the next move turns this integral into an average under $q$, and $q$ knows where the patch is.

Appendix · the ELBO through Jensen · move 3 of 4

Read the integral as an average under $q$

$$\log \int q_\varphi(z\mid x)\,\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\,dz$$
$$= \log\; \hl{\E_{z\sim q_\varphi(z\mid x)}}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\right]$$
From the $\E$ slide: $\int q(z)\,f(z)\,dz = \E_{z\sim q}[f(z)]$. Here $f(z)$ is the ratio $p_\theta(x,z)/q_\varphi(z\mid x)$: one number for each code.

Still exact. We now average over codes drawn from the encoder's cloud, right where the patch is.

Appendix · the ELBO through Jensen · a tool for move 4

Jensen's inequality, in one picture

$\log$ curves downwards (it is concave), so every chord lies below the curve.

Two values, $R=1$ and $R=4$:

$$\underbrace{\log 2.5}_{\log \text{ of the average}} = 0.916 \;\ge\; \underbrace{\tfrac12(0 + 1.386)}_{\text{average of the logs}} = 0.693$$
$$\log \E[R] \;\ge\; \E[\log R]$$

Read the picture: two orange points on the curve; their chord lies below the curve. The midpoint of the chord (average of logs) sits below the curve at the midpoint (log of the average).

Appendix · the ELBO through Jensen · move 4 of 4

Swap the log and the average: the only inequality

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \log\,\E_{q}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\right]$$
$$\log p_\theta(x) \;\hl{\ge}\; \E_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\right] \;=:\; \ELBO$$
Jensen with $R = p_\theta(x,z)/q_\varphi(z\mid x)$. The right side is the Evidence Lower BOund: it can never exceed $\log p_\theta(x)$.

The same ELBO as Part 5. This route shows it is a bound; Part 5's route also shows the gap is $\KL(q\,\|\,\text{posterior})$.
