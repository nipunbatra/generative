Step 2: of all the bell curves, take the one with the smallest KL to the posterior. That is variational inference.

q_b sits at z = log 4 = 1.386, where sigmoid(z) = 0.8: the data's favourite value, with a narrow width. It is confident and slightly wrong. q_a is the prior: right shape, wrong place. q_c is the best Gaussian (found by optimisation, next slides).

"Variational" comes from the calculus of variations: optimising over functions. Here the "function" q is pinned down by just two numbers.

The goal, written down

Find the $q$ with the smallest KL to the posterior

$$q^{\star} = \arg\min_{q\,\in\,\mathcal Q}\; \KL\big(\,\cenc{q(z)}\,\|\,\cx{p(z\mid x)}\,\big)$$

$\mathcal Q$: the shapes we allow

Gaussians $\N(m, s^2)$. Two numbers to find. (The VAE: a few dozen.)

Why $q$ first?

KL averages under its first argument (Part 4). We can draw from $q$; we cannot draw from the posterior (Part 3's catch).

What it will do

$q$ first means mode-seeking (Part 4's two bumps): $q$ will hide inside the posterior rather than spread over it.

Inference has become optimisation: two numbers, one objective. Before any maths, try it by hand.

Same formula as the PML course's VI slides: Ψ* = argmin KL(q_Ψ(θ) ‖ P(θ | D)), with Ψ = (m, s) and θ = z here.

The catch

The KL to the posterior contains the evidence

$$\KL\big(q\,\|\,p(z\mid x)\big) = \E_{z\sim q}\big[\log q(z) - \log p(z\mid x)\big]$$
$$p(z\mid x) = \frac{p(x, z)}{\hl{p(x)}}$$

To score $q$ this way we need $p(x)$: the integral of Part 2. We are back where we started.

Four short moves get the evidence out of the way. Watch where $\log p(x)$ goes.

From the KL to the ELBO · move 1 of 4

Write out the KL

$$\KL\big(q(z)\,\|\,p(z\mid x)\big)$$
$$= \E_{z\sim q}\big[\,\log q(z) - \hl{\log p(z\mid x)}\,\big]$$
The definition from Part 4: the average, under the first argument, of the log of the ratio. The highlighted term is the one we cannot compute.

From the KL to the ELBO · move 2 of 4

Bayes: the posterior is the joint over the evidence

$$\E_{q}\big[\log q(z) - \log p(z\mid x)\big]$$
$$= \E_{q}\big[\log q(z) - \log p(x,z)\big] \;+\; \hl{\log p(x)}$$
$\log p(z\mid x) = \log p(x,z) - \log p(x)$. The evidence has no $z$ in it: the same number for every draw, so it comes out of the average.

The joint $p(x,z) = p(z)\,p(x\mid z)$ is Part 2's one line of code. All the difficulty is now in one number.

From the KL to the ELBO · move 3 of 4

Move the evidence to the other side

$$\KL\big(q\,\|\,p(z\mid x)\big) = \E_{q}\big[\log q(z) - \log p(x,z)\big] + \log p(x)$$
$$\log p(x) = \underbrace{\hl{\E_{q}\big[\log p(x,z) - \log q(z)\big]}}_{\ELBO(q)} \;+\; \KL\big(q\,\|\,p(z\mid x)\big)$$
Rearrange, and give the highlighted average a name: the ELBO. Every piece of it is computable: the joint (one line of code) and $q$ (ours, so we can evaluate it and draw from it).

Why "Evidence Lower BOund"? The last move.

From the KL to the ELBO · move 4 of 4

The KL is never negative, so the ELBO is a lower bound

$$\log p(x) = \ELBO(q) + \underbrace{\KL\big(q\,\|\,p(z\mid x)\big)}_{\ge\, 0} \;\ge\; \ELBO(q)$$

Our coin, two choices of $q$. The right end, $\log p(x) = {{vik.coin.log_px}}$, is fixed by the model and the data: it does not move when $q$ changes.

Pause here: this is the single idea of variational inference. It is the picture in Bishop, PRML, Figure 10.2 (and the mbernste ELBO figure in the PML slides), with real numbers. Axis not to scale.

At q = prior the KL to the prior is 0, so the ELBO there is just the average log-likelihood of codes drawn from the prior: {{vik.vi.rec0}}.

Unpacking the ELBO · step 1 of 2

The joint is prior times likelihood

$$\ELBO(q) = \E_{q}\big[\log p(x,z) - \log q(z)\big]$$
$$= \E_{q}\Big[\hl{\log p(x\mid z) + \log p(z) - \log q(z)}\Big]$$
$$= \E_{q}\big[\log p(x\mid z)\big] \;+\; \E_{q}\big[\log p(z) - \log q(z)\big]$$
The log of a product is a sum of logs; the average of a sum is the sum of the averages. Group the likelihood on its own, and the two terms that only involve $z$ together.

Unpacking the ELBO · step 2 of 2

Recognise the second average: a KL to the prior

$$\E_{q}\big[\log p(z) - \log q(z)\big]$$
$$= -\,\E_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z)}\right] = \hl{-\KL\big(q(z)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}$$
Flip the sign to flip the fraction, then Part 4's definition. And for $q = \N(m, s^2)$, $p(z) = \N(0, 1)$, Part 4 already has the formula: $\tfrac12\big(m^2 + s^2 - 1 - \log s^2\big)$.

Two KLs now appear: to the posterior (the gap, not computable) and to the prior (inside the ELBO, by formula). Do not mix them up.

Put the pieces together

The ELBO, in symbols and in words

$$\ELBO = \underbrace{\E_{z\sim\cenc q}\big[\cdec{\log p(x\mid z)}\big]}_{\text{explain the data}} \;-\; \underbrace{\closs{\KL\big(q(z)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}}_{\text{stay close to the prior}}$$
For the coin: the average, over draws $z$ from $q$, of $8\log\mathrm{sigmoid}(z) + 2\log(1-\mathrm{sigmoid}(z))$.
For the coin: $\tfrac12\big(m^2 + s^2 - 1 - \log s^2\big)$, no sampling needed.

In the VAE these two terms get names: the reconstruction term and the KL term. Same two terms, a bigger $x$.
