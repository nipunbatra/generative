The ELBO: a loss we can actually compute

Goal: give our 3 high probability, as with the names. Problem: for a latent-variable model that probability is an integral nobody can evaluate. Watch it happen on our 3.

The model, as a picture

Two arrows: generate down, infer up

$$p_\theta(x, z) = \cz{p(z)}\;\cdec{p_\theta(x\mid z)}$$

Solid arrow: the generative model we want, prior times decoder. Dashed arrow: an approximate inference network we add only to make training possible. θ are decoder weights, φ are encoder weights.

Same story as the coin and the bumps; z is now a 2-D code and p(x|z) is a neural network.

Maximum likelihood for our 3

How probable is our 3 under the model?

With the coin we summed over two values:

$$p(x) = \sum_{z\in\{\mathrm A,\mathrm B\}} p(z)\,p(x\mid z)$$

A code can be any point in the plane, so the sum becomes an integral:

$$p_\theta(x) = \int \cz{p(z)}\;\cdec{p_\theta(x\mid z)}\,dz$$

2 numbers per code, so we can afford a 901 × 901 grid of decoder calls: $\log p_\theta(x) = {{evidence.log_px}}$ nats. With 16 numbers it would be $901^{16}$ calls.

The map shows log p(x | z) for our 3 at every code z: dark = codes that decode to something like our 3. Dashed circles: 1, 2, 3 prior standard deviations.

Real computation on the trained 2-D VAE decoder: 811,801 decoder calls, then log-sum-exp.

Idea: estimate the integral by sampling

Random codes almost never hit the right patch

$$p_\theta(x) = \E_{z\sim p(z)}\big[\,p_\theta(x\mid z)\,\big] \approx \frac1S\sum_{s=1}^{S} p_\theta(x\mid z_s)$$
100,000random codes $z_s\sim\N(0,I)$
{{evidence.prior_hits_2sd}}%land inside our 3's patch
{{evidence.llik_median_prior}}typical $\log p(x\mid z)$ of a random code
{{evidence.llik_best}}the best code's $\log p(x\mid z)$

A typical random code makes our 3 about $e^{243}$ times less probable than the best code. Blind sampling wastes almost every draw.

The estimate is unbiased but its variance is enormous: the average is dominated by the rare draws that hit the patch, and with 16 or 100 dimensions you would essentially never hit it.

Better: ask someone where the patch is. That "someone" is the encoder.

Inference, like the coin and the bumps

Ask the encoder where to look

The patch, normalised, is the true posterior:

$$p_\theta(z\mid x) = \frac{p(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)}{p_\theta(x)}$$

It needs $p_\theta(x)$, the impossible integral. So we train a network to guess it:

$$\cenc{q_\varphi(z\mid x)} = \N\big(\mu_\varphi(x),\, \sigma_\varphi(x)^2\big)$$

For our 3: $\mu = ({{our3.mu.0}},\ {{our3.mu.1}})$ and $\sigma = ({{our3.sigma.0}},\ {{our3.sigma.1}})$. Close to the truth, not identical.

Zoomed view around our 3. Shaded: the true posterior from the grid. Purple ellipses: one and two standard deviations of the encoder's Gaussian. The true posterior is slightly tilted and offset; a diagonal Gaussian cannot tilt.

Notice the scale: σ ≈ 0.05 while the prior has σ = 1. The encoder is very confident about where our 3 lives.

Deriving the ELBO · move 1 of 7

Start from what we want

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \log \int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz$$
Maximum likelihood wants this number large for our 3, and for every image in the data. The integral over all codes is the part we cannot compute.

We will rewrite this line until it becomes something we can compute and differentiate.

Recall p(x, z) = p(z) p(x | z): prior times decoder. Each of the next six slides makes exactly one change. Ask the class to name the move before you click.

Deriving the ELBO · move 2 of 7

Multiply and divide by the encoder's cloud

$$\log \int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz$$
$$= \log \int \hl{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\;\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{\hl{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}}\,dz$$
Multiplying and dividing by the same positive number changes nothing. We are free to choose any $q$; we choose the encoder's Gaussian, which is positive everywhere.

Why bother? Because the next move turns this integral into an average under $q$, and $q$ knows where the patch is.

Deriving the ELBO · move 3 of 7

Read the integral as an average under $q$

$$\log \int q_\varphi(z\mid x)\,\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\,dz$$
$$= \log\; \hl{\E_{z\sim q_\varphi(z\mid x)}}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\right]$$
From the $\E$ slide: $\int q(z)\,f(z)\,dz = \E_{z\sim q}[f(z)]$. Here $f(z)$ is the ratio $p_\theta(x,z)/q_\varphi(z\mid x)$: one number for each code.

Still exact. We now average over codes drawn from the encoder's cloud, right where the patch is.

Deriving the ELBO · a tool for move 4

Jensen's inequality, in one picture

$\log$ curves downwards (it is concave), so every chord lies below the curve.

Two values, $R=1$ and $R=4$:

$$\underbrace{\log 2.5}_{\log \text{ of the average}} = 0.916 \;\ge\; \underbrace{\tfrac12(0 + 1.386)}_{\text{average of the logs}} = 0.693$$
$$\log \E[R] \;\ge\; \E[\log R]$$

Read the picture: two orange points on the curve; their chord lies below the curve. The midpoint of the chord (average of logs) sits below the curve at the midpoint (log of the average).

Deriving the ELBO · move 4 of 7

Swap the log and the average: the only inequality

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \log\,\E_{q}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\right]$$
$$\log p_\theta(x) \;\hl{\ge}\; \E_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\right] \;=:\; \ELBO$$
Jensen with $R = p_\theta(x,z)/q_\varphi(z\mid x)$. The right side is the Evidence Lower BOund: it can never exceed $\log p_\theta(x)$.

The log is now inside the average. That is what makes the next three moves possible.

Deriving the ELBO · move 5 of 7

Split the log of a product

$$\ELBO = \E_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(x\mid z)\,p(z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\right]$$
$$= \E_{q}\Big[\hl{\log p_\theta(x\mid z) + \log p(z) - \log q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\Big]$$
First write $p_\theta(x,z) = p_\theta(x\mid z)\,p(z)$: decoder times prior. Then $\log\frac{ab}{c} = \log a + \log b - \log c$.

Deriving the ELBO · move 6 of 7

The average of a sum is the sum of the averages

$$\E_{q}\Big[\log p_\theta(x\mid z) + \log p(z) - \log q_\varphi(z\mid x)\Big]$$
$$= \hl{\E_{q}\big[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)\big]} \;+\; \hl{\E_{q}\big[\log p(z) - \log q_\varphi(z\mid x)\big]}$$
$\E[a + b] = \E[a] + \E[b]$. Group the decoder term on its own, and the two terms that only involve $z$ together.

Deriving the ELBO · move 7 of 7

Recognise the second average: it is a KL

$$\E_{q}\big[\log p(z) - \log q_\varphi(z\mid x)\big]$$
$$= -\,\E_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}{p(z)}\right] = \hl{-\KL\big(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}$$
Flip the sign to flip the fraction. Then use the definition: $\KL(q\|p) = \E_{z\sim q}\big[\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z)}\big]$, the average extra surprise.

And for Gaussians we already have its formula: $\tfrac12\sum_j(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-1-\log\sigma_j^2)$.

Put the pieces together

The ELBO, in symbols and in words

$$\ELBO = \underbrace{\E_{z\sim\cenc q}\big[\cx{\log p_\theta(x\mid z)}\big]}_{\text{rebuild } x \text{ from codes in its cloud}} \;-\; \underbrace{\closs{\KL\big(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}}_{\text{keep the cloud near } \N(0,I)}$$
First term: the autoencoder's reconstruction score, with the code drawn from the cloud.
Second term: the Gaussian KL we just derived.

The two wishes from the autoencoder slide, derived rather than invented. The loss we minimise is $-\ELBO$.

What did the inequality cost?

A second route: start from the true posterior

$\KL\big(q\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\big)$$=$$\E_q\big[\log q_\varphi(z\mid x) - \log p_\theta(z\mid x)\big]$ definition of KL, now against the true posterior $=$$\E_q\big[\log q_\varphi(z\mid x) - \log p_\theta(x,z)\big] + \log p_\theta(x)$ Bayes: $\log p_\theta(z\mid x) = \log p_\theta(x,z) - \log p_\theta(x)$, and $\log p_\theta(x)$ has no $z$ in it $=$$-\ELBO + \log p_\theta(x)$ the average is exactly minus the ELBO from move 4
$$\log p_\theta(x) = \ELBO + \KL\big(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\big)$$

The gap is a KL, so it is never negative: a second proof that the ELBO is a lower bound.

The reconstruction term, zoomed in

Each pixel is a coin flip with probability $\pi_i$

our 3, one 5 × 5 patch

$$-\log p_\theta(x\mid z) = -\sum_{i=1}^{784}\Big[x_i\log\pi_i + (1-x_i)\log(1-\pi_i)\Big] = {{our3.recon}}\ \text{nats}$$

Binary cross-entropy is not a design choice: it is the Bernoulli log-likelihood. Gaussian pixels would give squared error.

The patch is on the top stroke of our 3. Where x = 1 the loss is −log π; where x = 0 it is −log(1 − π). Ink predicted at 0.89 costs 0.11; ink predicted at 0.18 costs 1.71.

In code we pass logits to binary_cross_entropy_with_logits for numerical stability; π = sigmoid(logits) is only for display.

From theory to the training loss

The VAE loss for our 3

$$\mathcal L(x) = -\ELBO \approx \underbrace{\closs{-\log p_\theta(x\mid z)}}_{\text{BCE over 784 pixels}} \;+\; \underbrace{\closs{\tfrac12\sum_{j=1}^{2}\big(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-1-\log\sigma_j^2\big)}}_{\text{KL over 2 coordinates}},\qquad z\sim q_\varphi(z\mid x)$$
{{our3.recon}}reconstruction, one draw of $z$
+ {{our3.kl_total}}KL, exact formula
= {{our3.loss}}loss for our 3

One problem left: $z$ is a random draw, and we need gradients through it.

Using a single z per image per step is standard. The reconstruction term is a one-sample estimate of the expectation; the KL term is computed exactly.
