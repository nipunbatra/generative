Part 5 of 7

Variational inference, and the ELBO

Part 3 wanted a simple $q$ close to the posterior of our 3. Part 4 gave a number for "close". Write the goal down: one rearrangement turns it into a loss we can compute, the ELBO. Then run it, and see why it leads to an encoder.

The goal, written down

Find the $q$ with the smallest KL to the posterior

$$q^{\star} = \arg\min_{q\,\in\,\mathcal Q}\; \KL\big(\,q(z)\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\,\big)$$

$\mathcal Q$: the shapes we allow

Diagonal Gaussians. For our 2-number code: 4 numbers, a centre $m$ and two widths $s$.

Why $q$ first?

KL averages under its first argument. $q$ is ours, so we can draw from it. The other order would need draws from the posterior: the thing we cannot get.

The catch

$p_\theta(z\mid x) = p_\theta(x,z)\,/\,p_\theta(x)$, and $p_\theta(x)$ is the integral we cannot do. We cannot even evaluate this KL.

Four short moves get $p_\theta(x)$ out of the way.

This is KL(q ‖ p), the "reverse" or mode-seeking direction from the end of Part 4. Here the choice is forced by practicality: we can only average under a distribution we can sample.

From the KL to the ELBO · move 1 of 4

Write out the KL

$$\KL\big(q(z)\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\big)$$
$$= \E_{z\sim q}\big[\,\log q(z) - \hl{\log p_\theta(z\mid x)}\,\big]$$
The definition from Part 4: the average, under the first argument, of the log of the ratio. The highlighted term is the one we cannot compute.

From the KL to the ELBO · move 2 of 4

Bayes: the posterior is the joint over the evidence

$$\E_{q}\big[\log q(z) - \log p_\theta(z\mid x)\big]$$
$$= \E_{q}\big[\log q(z) - \log p_\theta(x,z)\big] \;+\; \hl{\log p_\theta(x)}$$
$\log p_\theta(z\mid x) = \log p_\theta(x,z) - \log p_\theta(x)$. The evidence has no $z$ in it: the same number for every draw, so it comes out of the average.

The joint $p_\theta(x,z) = p(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)$ is easy: prior times decoder. All the difficulty is now in one number.

From the KL to the ELBO · move 3 of 4

Move the evidence to the other side

$$\KL\big(q\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\big) = \E_{q}\big[\log q(z) - \log p_\theta(x,z)\big] + \log p_\theta(x)$$
$$\log p_\theta(x) = \underbrace{\hl{\E_{q}\big[\log p_\theta(x,z) - \log q(z)\big]}}_{\ELBO(q)} \;+\; \KL\big(q\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\big)$$
Rearrange, and give the highlighted average a name: the ELBO. Every piece of it is computable: the joint is prior times decoder, and $q$ is ours.

Why "Evidence Lower BOund"? The last move.

From the KL to the ELBO · move 4 of 4

The KL is never negative, so the ELBO is a lower bound

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \ELBO(q) + \underbrace{\KL\big(q\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\big)}_{\ge\, 0} \;\ge\; \ELBO(q)$$

Our 3, two choices of $q$. The right end, $\log p_\theta(x)$, is fixed by the model; it does not move when $q$ changes.

The total cannot move, so raising the ELBO must shrink the KL: maximise the ELBO and $q$ approaches the posterior, without ever computing $p_\theta(x)$.

Pause here: this is the single idea of variational inference. It is the picture in Bishop, PRML, Figure 10.2, with real numbers. Axis not to scale: the second gap is about 3,000 times smaller than the first.

At q = prior the KL to the prior is 0, so the ELBO there is just the average log p(x | z) of random codes: the blind sampling of Part 3. The next slides find the second q.

Unpacking the ELBO · step 1 of 3

The joint is prior times decoder

$$\ELBO(q) = \E_{q}\big[\log p_\theta(x,z) - \log q(z)\big]$$
$$= \E_{q}\Big[\hl{\log p_\theta(x\mid z) + \log p(z) - \log q(z)}\Big]$$
$p_\theta(x,z) = p(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)$: the generative story from Part 3. The log of a product is a sum of logs.

Why unpack? To see which parts are the decoder and which are the prior, and to get two terms with names.

Unpacking the ELBO · step 2 of 3

The average of a sum is the sum of the averages

$$\E_{q}\Big[\log p_\theta(x\mid z) + \log p(z) - \log q(z)\Big]$$
$$= \hl{\E_{q}\big[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)\big]} \;+\; \hl{\E_{q}\big[\log p(z) - \log q(z)\big]}$$
$\E[a + b] = \E[a] + \E[b]$. Group the decoder term on its own, and the two terms that only involve $z$ together.

Unpacking the ELBO · step 3 of 3

Recognise the second average: a KL to the prior

$$\E_{q}\big[\log p(z) - \log q(z)\big]$$
$$= -\,\E_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z)}\right] = \hl{-\KL\big(q(z)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}$$
Flip the sign to flip the fraction. Then use the definition: $\KL(q\|p) = \E_{z\sim q}\big[\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z)}\big]$, the average extra surprise.

And for Gaussians we already have its formula: $\tfrac12\sum_j(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-1-\log\sigma_j^2)$.

Put the pieces together

The ELBO, in symbols and in words

$$\ELBO = \underbrace{\E_{z\sim\cenc q}\big[\cdec{\log p_\theta(x\mid z)}\big]}_{\text{rebuild } x \text{ from codes in its cloud}} \;-\; \underbrace{\closs{\KL\big(q(z)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}}_{\text{keep the cloud near } \N(0,I)}$$
First term: the autoencoder's reconstruction score, with the code drawn from the cloud.
Second term: the Gaussian KL from Part 4, in closed form.

The two wishes from the end of Part 2, derived rather than invented: rebuild well, and keep codes where the prior can find them.

Variational inference by hand, for our 3

Four numbers, gradient ascent on the ELBO

x = our_3                                  # 784 pixels, fixed
m     = torch.zeros(2, requires_grad=True)   # q's centre
log_s = torch.zeros(2, requires_grad=True)   # q's log-widths
opt = torch.optim.Adam([m, log_s], lr=0.03)

for step in range(800):
    z = m + log_s.exp() * torch.randn(64, 2)    # 64 draws from q
    rec = -bce(decoder(z), x).sum(1).mean()    # E_q[log p(x|z)]
    kl  = 0.5 * (m**2 + (2*log_s).exp() - 1 - 2*log_s).sum()
    loss = -(rec - kl)                           # minus the ELBO
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

Only $q$ is learned. The decoder is a trained one, kept fixed; $x$ is our 3. Start at $q = \N(0, I)$: $m = 0$, $s = 1$.

Draw codes from $q$: centre plus width times standard noise, exactly how Part 1 sampled from a bump. Part 6 explains why gradients flow through this line.

The two terms: reconstruction, averaged over the 64 draws; the KL to the prior by the Gaussian formula from Part 4.

A bonus: with $q$, even $p_\theta(x)$ becomes easy to estimate

Sample codes where the patch is, then reweight

$$p_\theta(x) = \int q(z)\,\frac{p(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)}{q(z)}\,dz = \E_{z\sim q}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{q(z)}\right] \approx \frac1S\sum_{s=1}^{S}\frac{p_\theta(x, z_s)}{q(z_s)}$$

{{isamp.reps}} runs of 1,000 draws each. From the prior, the estimates scatter from {{isamp.prior_min}} to {{isamp.prior_max}}. From $q$: {{isamp.q_mean}}, give or take {{isamp.q_sd}}.

This is importance sampling: multiply and divide by q, then average under q. With 2 numbers per code, blind sampling from the prior is only noisy; with 16 it would almost never land in the patch (Part 3).

Put a log in front of this average and push it inside the average: that is the Jensen route to the ELBO in the appendix.

800 steps for one image

Amortised inference: learn to predict $q$

Our 3: ELBO {{vi.elbo2}} by hand in 800 steps, {{vi.elbo_enc2}} from the encoder in one pass. Pay once during training; a little worse, thousands of times cheaper.

The encoder outputs q_φ(z | x) = N(μ_φ(x), diag σ_φ(x)²). The small loss in ELBO is called the amortisation gap. Both numbers: the ELBO of our 3 under each q, 20,000 draws.

Over the first {{amort.n}} test digits the median gap is {{amort.median}} nats. Per-image VI from N(0, I) is itself a local search: on one digit it ended {{amort.min}} nats relative to the encoder (stuck), on another {{amort.max}} (the encoder was far off). Real comparisons use the encoder to start VI.

Put the pieces together

Encoder + decoder + ELBO = the variational autoencoder

$$\max_{\theta,\,\varphi}\;\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big(\E_{z\sim\cenc{q_\varphi(z\mid x_i)}}\big[\cdec{\log p_\theta(x_i\mid z)}\big] - \closs{\KL\big(q_\varphi(z\mid x_i)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}\Big)$$

"Variational"

$q$ always depended on the image; now we write it $q_\varphi(z\mid x)$: the best Gaussian under the ELBO, predicted by a network.

"Autoencoder"

image → encoder → code → decoder → image: the shape from Part 2, now with a cloud for a code.

Trained together

Pushing on $\theta$ raises the ELBO mostly by making the data more probable: a better model. Pushing on $\varphi$ shrinks the gap: a better $q$.

Kingma & Welling (2014) and Rezende et al. (2014): one network for the likelihood, one for the posterior, one loss for both.

What we did, and what comes next

What we did

  1. Goal: minimise $\KL(q\,\|\,\text{posterior})$. It contains $p_\theta(x)$.
  2. Rearranged: $\log p_\theta(x) = \ELBO + \KL$. Maximising the ELBO minimises the KL.
  3. ELBO = reconstruction $-\ \KL(q\,\|\,\text{prior})$: both computable.
  4. VI by hand fits our 3's $q$ in 800 steps; an encoder predicts it in one pass. That is the VAE.
