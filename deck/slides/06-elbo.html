Variational inference for our 3, then for every image

The VI lecture turned "make $q$ close to the posterior" into a loss we can compute, the ELBO, and fitted a coin with it (its Part 6). Here the unknown is our 3's code: run the same loss on it, read the numbers, and see why doing this image by image leads to an encoder.

The same identity, written for one image

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \underbrace{\E_{z\sim q}\big[\log p_\theta(x, z) - \log q(z)\big]}_{\ELBO(q)} + \underbrace{\KL\big(q\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\big)}_{\ge\, 0}$$

The ELBO needs only the joint, $p_\theta(x, z) = p(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)$: prior times decoder. No $p_\theta(x)$, no posterior.

$\log p_\theta(x)$ does not move when $q$ does, so whatever the ELBO gains, the KL to the posterior loses.

Careful with one letter: there, $\theta$ was the unknown; here it names the decoder's weights, and the unknown is $z$.

VI lecture (vi.html), Part 6: the catch · substitute Bayes · the evidence comes out · the lower bound · ELBO + KL for the coin

The VI lecture's three steps: write the KL to the posterior as an average under q, substitute Bayes' rule for the posterior, and take log p(x) out of the average because it does not depend on z. The identity holds for every q.

Ask: which of the two terms can we compute? The ELBO: draws from q, and the prior and decoder at those draws. The KL to the posterior needs p(x).

The lower bound, measured on our 3

The KL is never negative, so the ELBO sits below $\log p_\theta(x)$

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \ELBO(q) + \underbrace{\KL\big(q\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\big)}_{\ge\, 0} \;\ge\; \ELBO(q)$$

Our 3, two choices of $q$. The right end, $\log p_\theta(x)$, is fixed by the model; it does not move when $q$ changes.

The total cannot move, so raising the ELBO must shrink the KL: maximise the ELBO and $q$ approaches the posterior, without ever computing $p_\theta(x)$.

The VI lecture drew this picture for the coin (Part 6, three Beta q's); here it is with our 3's numbers. It is the picture in Bishop, PRML, Figure 10.2. Axis not to scale: the second gap is about 3,000 times smaller than the first.

At q = prior the KL to the prior is 0, so the ELBO there is just the average log p(x | z) of random codes: the blind sampling of Part 3. The next slides find the second q.

The VI lecture's second form, for an image

The ELBO, in symbols and in words

$$\ELBO = \E_{q}\big[\log p_\theta(x\mid z) + \log p(z) - \log q(z)\big]$$
$$\ELBO = \underbrace{\E_{z\sim\cenc q}\big[\cdec{\log p_\theta(x\mid z)}\big]}_{\text{rebuild } x \text{ from codes in its cloud}} \;-\; \underbrace{\closs{\KL\big(q(z)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}}_{\text{keep the cloud near } \N(0,I)}$$
First term: the autoencoder's reconstruction score, with the code drawn from the cloud.
Second term: the Gaussian KL to the prior from Part 4, in closed form.

The two wishes from the end of Part 2, derived rather than invented: rebuild well, and keep codes where the prior can find them.

VI lecture (vi.html), Part 6: two ways to write the same ELBO

The faded line splits the joint into decoder times prior. Group log p(z) with −log q(z): their average is minus the KL from q to the prior. It is the prior here, not the posterior, so both terms are computable.

Variational inference by hand, for our 3

Four numbers, gradient ascent on the ELBO

x = our_3                                  # 784 pixels, fixed
m     = torch.zeros(2, requires_grad=True)   # q's centre
log_s = torch.zeros(2, requires_grad=True)   # q's log-widths
opt = torch.optim.Adam([m, log_s], lr=0.03)

for step in range(800):
    z = m + log_s.exp() * torch.randn(64, 2)    # 64 draws from q
    rec = -bce(decoder(z), x).sum(1).mean()    # E_q[log p(x|z)]
    kl  = 0.5 * (m**2 + (2*log_s).exp() - 1 - 2*log_s).sum()
    loss = -(rec - kl)                           # minus the ELBO
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

Only $q$ is learned. The decoder is a trained one, kept fixed; $x$ is our 3. Start at $q = \N(0, I)$: $m = 0$, $s = 1$.

Draw codes from $q$: centre plus width times standard noise: the VI lecture's reparameterised draw (its Part 5), so gradients reach m and log_s.

The two terms: reconstruction, averaged over the 64 draws; the KL to the prior by the Gaussian formula from Part 4.

The same three-line loop as fitting one Gaussian in Part 1. The ELBO rises from {{vi.start_elbo}} to {{vi.elbo}}.

Real run (scripts/make_figures_extra.py) on the 2-number VAE decoder from Part 7, held fixed. The VAE encoder of Part 7 outputs logvar instead of log_s: logvar = 2 · log_s, the same information. Final q: centre ({{vi.mu.0}}, {{vi.mu.1}}), widths {{vi.sigma.0}} and {{vi.sigma.1}}. bce is binary_cross_entropy_with_logits with reduction="none".

A bonus: with $q$, even $p_\theta(x)$ becomes easy to estimate

Sample codes where the patch is, then reweight

$$p_\theta(x) = \int q(z)\,\frac{p(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)}{q(z)}\,dz = \E_{z\sim q}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{q(z)}\right] \approx \frac1S\sum_{s=1}^{S}\frac{p_\theta(x, z_s)}{q(z_s)}$$

{{isamp.reps}} runs of 1,000 draws each. From the prior, the estimates scatter from {{isamp.prior_min}} to {{isamp.prior_max}}. From $q$: {{isamp.q_mean}}, give or take {{isamp.q_sd}}.

This is importance sampling: multiply and divide by q, then average under q. With 2 numbers per code, blind sampling from the prior is only noisy; with 16 it would almost never land in the patch (Part 3).

Put a log in front of this average and push it inside the average: that is the Jensen route to the ELBO in the appendix.

800 steps for one image

Amortised inference: learn to predict $q$

Our 3: ELBO {{vi.elbo2}} by hand in 800 steps, {{vi.elbo_enc2}} from the encoder in one pass. Pay once during training; a little worse, thousands of times cheaper.

The encoder outputs q_φ(z | x) = N(μ_φ(x), diag σ_φ(x)²). The small loss in ELBO is called the amortisation gap. Both numbers: the ELBO of our 3 under each q, 20,000 draws.

Over the first {{amort.n}} test digits the median gap is {{amort.median}} nats. Per-image VI from N(0, I) is itself a local search: on one digit it ended {{amort.min}} nats relative to the encoder (stuck), on another {{amort.max}} (the encoder was far off). Real comparisons use the encoder to start VI.

Put the pieces together

Encoder + decoder + ELBO = the variational autoencoder

$$\max_{\theta,\,\varphi}\;\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big(\E_{z\sim\cenc{q_\varphi(z\mid x_i)}}\big[\cdec{\log p_\theta(x_i\mid z)}\big] - \closs{\KL\big(q_\varphi(z\mid x_i)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}\Big)$$

"Variational"

$q$ always depended on the image; now we write it $q_\varphi(z\mid x)$: the best Gaussian under the ELBO, predicted by a network.

"Autoencoder"

image → encoder → code → decoder → image: the shape from Part 2, now with a cloud for a code.

Trained together

Pushing on $\theta$ raises the ELBO mostly by making the data more probable: a better model. Pushing on $\varphi$ shrinks the gap: a better $q$.

Kingma & Welling (2014) and Rezende et al. (2014): one network for the likelihood, one for the posterior, one loss for both.
