A reminder: prior, likelihood, posterior

Variational inference approximates exactly one thing: a posterior. So before any new maths, rebuild Bayes' rule on the simplest data there is: ten tosses of a coin.

The data

Ten tosses: 8 heads, 2 tails

HHTHHHHTHH

What is this coin's chance of heads? Call it $\theta$.

The obvious answer: $8/10 = 0.8$. That is the maximum-likelihood estimate.

But ten tosses do not rule out $\theta = 0.7$, or even $0.5$. We want a whole distribution over $\theta$: how strongly we believe each value.

Bayes' rule turns "what I believed before" and "what I saw" into "what I believe now".

Call the ten tosses x. Order does not matter for the maths, but keep the sequence on screen: every number in the next slides is about exactly this sequence.

Ask: would you bet even money that the coin is fair? Most say no. Would you bet your house that θ is exactly 0.8? Also no. That in-between state of belief is what a posterior is.

Add up the joint column: ${{vik.three.ev}}\times10^{-4}$. Keep this number; it has a name.

Posterior = joint ÷ sum. The coin with $\theta = 0.8$ now has 87% of our belief. The sum we divided by is $p(x)$, the evidence.

Likelihood of the 0.8 coin: 0.8^8 × 0.2^2 = 0.16777 × 0.04 = 0.00671. (The same product appears in the KL betting game later, by design.) The 0.5 coin: 0.5^10 = 0.000977. The 0.2 coin: 0.2^8 × 0.8^2 = 1.6 × 10⁻⁶.

Joint = likelihood / 3. Posterior: 22.37 / 25.63 = 0.873, 3.26 / 25.63 = 0.127, and 0.0002 for the 0.2 coin.

Point at the division: without it the joint column does not add up to 1. That is the only job of the evidence, and it is the thing that becomes impossible later.

The same table, in symbols

Bayes' rule, and the troublemaker at the bottom

$$\underbrace{\cx{p(\theta\mid x)}}_{\text{posterior}} \;=\; \frac{\overbrace{\cz{p(\theta)}}^{\text{prior}}\;\overbrace{\cdec{p(x\mid\theta)}}^{\text{likelihood}}}{\underbrace{p(x)}_{\text{evidence}}}$$
$$p(x) = \sum_{\theta\,\in\,\{0.2,\,0.5,\,0.8\}} p(\theta)\,p(x\mid\theta) \qquad \text{three coins: a sum of three terms}$$
$$p(x) = \int_0^1 p(\theta)\,p(x\mid\theta)\,d\theta \qquad \text{every } \theta \text{ between 0 and 1: an integral}$$

The evidence is the joint, added up over every possible value of the hidden quantity. Watch it: it is the whole difficulty.

Top: read it as "posterior ∝ prior × likelihood, then rescale". The evidence does not depend on θ: it is one number for the data set, the same in every row of the table.

The colours stay fixed for the whole lecture: orange = prior, teal = likelihood, blue = posterior (and later, purple = our approximation q).

Every value of $\theta$ at once

Prior × likelihood, rescaled: the posterior

$\times$ $\propto$

Multiply the two curves point by point, rescale to area 1. Posterior mean {{vik.beta.mean}}: between the prior's 0.5 and the data's 0.8.

The posterior's exponents just add: θ¹(1−θ)¹ from the prior times θ⁸(1−θ)² from the data gives θ⁹(1−θ)³, which is Beta(10, 4). Mean 10/14 = 0.714, mode 9/12 = 0.75, standard deviation {{vik.beta.sd}}.

"∝" means "proportional to": equal up to the rescaling, which is exactly the division by p(x).

Each head multiplies in one more $\theta$, each tail one more $(1-\theta)$. More tosses: a narrower posterior.

Start from "no tosses" (posterior = prior), then click heads and tails. After 40 tosses the posterior is narrow whatever the prior was: the data win.

Why the posterior stays a Beta: the prior and the likelihood have the same form, θ to a power times (1 − θ) to a power. That luck is called conjugacy, and it is what disappears in Part 2.

For this prior, we got lucky

The evidence has a formula

$$p(x) = \int_0^1 \underbrace{\cz{6\theta(1-\theta)}}_{\text{prior}}\;\underbrace{\cdec{\theta^8(1-\theta)^2}}_{\text{likelihood}}\,d\theta = 6\int_0^1 \theta^9(1-\theta)^3\,d\theta = 6\,B(10, 4)$$

$B(a, b)$, the Beta function, is a tabulated integral: $B(10, 4) = \dfrac{9!\;3!}{13!}$. So $p(x) = {{vik.beta.ev}}$ and $\log p(x) = {{vik.beta.log_ev}}$.

The integrand is a power of $\theta$ times a power of $(1-\theta)$: the same form as the prior. That is conjugacy. It is rare, and it is the only reason this integral could be done.

Change the prior slightly and the luck runs out. That is Part 2.

Check: 9! 3! / 13! = 362,880 × 6 / 6,227,020,800 = 3.4965 × 10⁻⁴; times 6 is 0.0020979. In logs, −6.167 nats. Compare with the three-coin table's 25.63 × 10⁻⁴: different, because there the prior allowed only three coins.

Note that x here is the exact sequence of ten tosses, so there is no binomial coefficient.

The VAE needs a posterior for every training image, and it never has a formula. This lecture builds the tool for that.

Why does training need the posterior? To improve the decoder for one image, you need to know which codes that image came from: exactly like fitting two bumps needed "which bump did this point come from". And maximum likelihood needs log p(x), the evidence.
