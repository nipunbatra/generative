Two numbers and beyond: where a simple $q$ falls short

The VAE's $q$ is a Gaussian with one width per coordinate, and no tilt. Fit that kind of $q$ to shapes in a plane, and see exactly what it gets wrong, and why.

A tilted target, a $q$ that cannot tilt

Mean-field $q$ on a correlated Gaussian: too narrow

$$q(z_1, z_2) = \N(z_1;\,m_1, s_1^2)\;\N(z_2;\,m_2, s_2^2)$$

One width per coordinate, independent under $q$: mean-field. Target: correlation $\rho = 0.9$, each coordinate's spread 1.

Left, VI's direction: $q$ must not put mass where $p$ is thin, so it shrinks inside the ellipse. Width $\sqrt{1-\rho^2} = {{vik.corr.mf_sd}}$, not 1.

Right, the other direction: wide enough to cover, width 1, but lots of mass where $p$ has none.

Mean-field VI underestimates uncertainty. Both fits pay the same KL here, {{vik.corr.rev_kl}} nats, by opposite mistakes.

This is Bishop, PRML, Figure 10.2. Blue: the target. Purple: q's 1- and 2-standard-deviation ellipses. Closed form: the mean-field optimum of reverse KL has precision equal to the target's diagonal precision, 1/(1 − ρ²), so variance 1 − ρ² = {{vik.corr.mf_var}}.

Where tilted posteriors come from: Bayesian linear regression's intercept and slope (the PML course's last VI example), or two pixels of a code that trade off against each other.

The coincidence of equal KLs: both are −½ log(1 − ρ²) = 0.830 for this target.

Give $q$ a tilt

A full-covariance $q$ fits the tilted target exactly

$$z = m + L\eps,\quad L = \begin{pmatrix} \ell_{11} & 0 \\ \ell_{21} & \ell_{22}\end{pmatrix},\quad \eps\sim\N(0, I)$$

Reparameterisation in 2-D: one extra number, $\ell_{21}$, lets $q$ tilt.

Both fitted by the same loop (reverse KL, rsample, Adam). Diagonal: KL {{vik.corr.fit_diag_kl}}. Full: KL {{vik.corr.fit_full_kl}}, correlation {{vik.corr.fit_full_rho}}.

With $d$ numbers in $z$: $d$ widths for a diagonal $q$, $d(d+1)/2$ for a full one. The VAE keeps diagonal: cheaper, and its decoder adapts.

L is the Cholesky factor: q's covariance is L Lᵀ. The two diagonal entries are learned as logs (positive widths); ℓ21 can be any number. For 32 numbers, a full covariance needs 528 numbers per data point instead of 32.

A curved target

A Gaussian can stretch and tilt, but it cannot bend

A banana: $z_1 \sim \N(0, 1.2^2)$, then $z_2$ centred on the curve $0.6\,z_1^2 - 1.2$.

Diagonal $q$: KL {{vik.banana.diag_kl}}. Full $q$: KL {{vik.banana.full_kl}}. The same.

The banana is symmetric, so the best tilt is no tilt. Both $q$'s sit in the bend and miss the arms.

More parameters of the same kind do not help. The family is the limit, not the optimiser.

Two separate bumps, in a plane

Reverse KL picks one bump, and pays $\log 2$

Start a little right of centre: the upper bump, KL {{vik.bumps2.kl_a}}. A little left: the lower bump, KL {{vik.bumps2.kl_b}}. Both $\approx \log 2 = {{vik.bumps2.log2}}$.

$q$ fits half of $p$ perfectly and ignores the other half: $\log 2$ is the price of missing half the mass. The forward KL covers both, through the empty middle.

Why exactly log 2: if q equals one bump, then p(z) ≈ ½ q(z) wherever q has mass, so log q − log p ≈ log 2 everywhere q looks. Part 4's 1-D two bumps, now in a plane. In a VAE this is a posterior where two quite different codes explain an image equally well.

Back to one number

The gap is the KL that no Gaussian can remove

Skewed target: the best Gaussian, $\N({{vik.shapes.skew_m}},\ {{vik.shapes.skew_s}}^2)$, leaves {{vik.shapes.skew_kl}} nats.

Two bumps: the best Gaussian is one bump, $\N({{vik.shapes.mix_m}},\ {{vik.shapes.mix_s}}^2)$, and leaves {{vik.shapes.mix_kl}} nats $= \log 2$.

The ELBO can never reach $\log p(x)$ here: the gap stays at least this big, whatever the optimiser does.

Both fits by brute-force search over (m, s) on a grid, minimising KL(q ‖ p). The coin's posterior was only slightly skewed, so its gap was 0.002; this skew-normal target (shape parameter 5) is strongly skewed.

The fix: a richer $q$

Push simple noise through a curve: a flow

$z = f(\eps)$ with $\eps \sim \N(0, 1)$ and $f$ monotone. The right curve makes $q$ match the skewed target exactly.

Normalizing flows learn $f$ as an invertible neural network, so $\log q(z)$ stays computable. Reparameterisation still works: $z$ is a differentiable function of $\eps$.

Richer families (flows, mixtures) shrink the gap; the ELBO and the loop do not change at all.

Honest note: this f was not learned. It is the exact map that turns 𝒩(0, 1) into the target (the target's inverse CDF applied to the normal CDF), computed numerically to show what a flow aims for. A trained flow approximates such a map. Change of variables: log q(z) = log 𝒩(ε) − log |f′(ε)|.
