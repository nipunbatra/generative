KL divergence: how far apart are two distributions?

The fix for the autoencoder was to keep each image's cloud of codes close to $\N(0, I)$. To say close we need one number for the gap between two distributions. We build it from two bars.

Two distributions over the same two outcomes

A dataset of 1s and 7s, 80% 1s. The model believes 60/40. How wrong is it, as one number?

Keep the story concrete: draw digits from the data, ask the model how probable each was. When a 1 shows up, the model said 0.6 when it should have said 0.8.

Ingredient: surprise

Surprise $= -\log(\text{probability})$

outcome$P$$Q$surprise if you believe $P$surprise if you believe $Q$
a 10.80.6$-\log 0.8 = 0.223$$-\log 0.6 = 0.511$
a 70.20.4$-\log 0.2 = 1.609$$-\log 0.4 = 0.916$
Certain events cost 0. Rare events cost a lot. Natural logs, so the unit is the nat.
Believing $Q$, a 1 surprises us more than it should ($0.511$ vs $0.223$), a 7 less ($0.916$ vs $1.609$).

This is the same −log p that appeared in the name model and in cross-entropy. Nothing new, just a name for it.

KL, step 1 of 3

For each outcome: how much extra surprise does $Q$ cause?

$$\underbrace{-\log Q_i}_{\text{surprise under } Q} \;-\; \underbrace{(-\log P_i)}_{\text{surprise under } P} \;=\; \log\frac{P_i}{Q_i}$$
outcome$-\log Q_i$$-\log P_i$extra surprise $\log(P_i/Q_i)$
a 10.5110.223$0.511 - 0.223 = 0.288$
a 70.9161.609$0.916 - 1.609 = -0.693$

Negative for the 7: $Q$ over-predicts 7s, so a 7 surprises it less than it should.

KL, step 2 of 3

Weight each outcome by how often it really happens

outcomehow often: $P_i$extra surpriseweighted: $P_i\log(P_i/Q_i)$
a 10.80.288$0.8 \times 0.288 = 0.230$
a 70.2$-0.693$$0.2 \times (-0.693) = -0.139$

Real data is 80% 1s, so the 1 row counts four times as much as the 7 row.

Why weight by P and not by Q? Because P is what actually happens. We ask: on the data we really see, how much extra surprise does the model cause per example?

KL, step 3 of 3

Add them up: that is the KL divergence

$$\KL(P\,\|\,Q) \;=\; \sum_i P_i \log\frac{P_i}{Q_i}$$
$$= 0.230 + (-0.139) = 0.092 \text{ nats}$$
One row was negative, yet the total can never be: $\KL \ge 0$.
$\KL = 0$ only when $Q = P$ exactly.

KL is the average extra surprise you pay for believing $Q$ when the truth is $P$.

Why can't the total be negative? To over-predict 7s, Q had to under-predict 1s, and 1s carry more weight. Gibbs' inequality makes this precise.

Notation we will use all lecture

$\E$ means "average over"

$$\E_{x\sim P}\big[f(x)\big] = \sum_x P(x)\,f(x)$$
exact, for a sum over outcomes
$$\E_{z\sim q}\big[f(z)\big] = \int q(z)\,f(z)\,dz$$
exact, for a continuous variable
$$\E_{x\sim P}\big[f(x)\big] \approx \frac1S\sum_{s=1}^{S} f(x_s),\quad x_s\sim P$$
estimated from samples
KL is an average: $\KL(P\|Q) = \E_{x\sim P}\big[\log\tfrac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\big]$. Five draws 1, 1, 7, 1, 1 give $\tfrac15(4\times 0.288 - 0.693) = 0.092$.

The subscript says which distribution the average is taken under: x ∼ P means "x is drawn from P". This matters because KL changes when you swap it.

Those five draws happen to be exactly 80/20, so the estimate matches. With random draws it would fluctuate around 0.092.

Not a distance

Swap the arguments, get a different number

$$\KL(Q\|P) = 0.6\log\frac{0.6}{0.8} + 0.4\log\frac{0.4}{0.2}$$
$$= -0.173 + 0.277 = 0.105$$

$0.105 \neq 0.092$: KL is not symmetric. "How wrong is $Q$ about $P$" differs from "how wrong is $P$ about $Q$".

Push $q \to 0$ and $\KL(P\|Q) \to \infty$. Never give zero probability to something that happens.

model's $q$ =0.60 $\KL(P\|Q)$ = 0.092$\KL(Q\|P)$ = 0.105

You have minimised a KL many times

Cross-entropy = entropy + KL

$H(P,Q)$$=$$\displaystyle -\sum_i P_i\log Q_i$ cross-entropy: the loss you train classifiers with $=$$\displaystyle -\sum_i P_i\log P_i \;+\; \sum_i P_i\big(\log P_i - \log Q_i\big)$ add and subtract $\sum_i P_i\log P_i$ $=$$\displaystyle \underbrace{H(P)}_{\text{entropy: fixed by the data}} \;+\; \KL(P\,\|\,Q)$
$H(P) = 0.500$does not depend on the model
$0.092$KL

Lowering cross-entropy can only lower the KL part. So does maximum likelihood: it minimises $\KL(\text{data}\,\|\,\text{model})$.

Together: H(P, Q) = 0.592 nats. Remember the direction: data first. That direction will explain why VAEs blur.

The version the VAE needs

KL between two bumps: an area

For continuous variables the sum becomes an integral:

$$\KL(q\|p) = \int q(z)\,\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z)}\,dz$$

Take $q = \N(1, 0.5^2)$, the encoder's cloud, and $p = \N(0,1)$, the prior.

The shaded area, computed numerically, is 0.818 nats.

Can we get 0.818 without a computer? Yes: for two Gaussians the integral has a formula. Three steps.

Numerical integral of the lower curve: {{kl_gauss_numeric}}. The grey part is where q is below p and contributes negatively, like the 7 row earlier.

Gaussian KL, step 1 of 2

Write out the log of the ratio

$\log q(z)$$=$$\displaystyle -\log\sigma - \tfrac12\log 2\pi - \frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$ log of $\N(z;\mu,\sigma^2)$ $\log p(z)$$=$$\displaystyle -\tfrac12\log 2\pi - \frac{z^2}{2}$ log of $\N(z;0,1)$ $\log\dfrac{q(z)}{p(z)}$$=$$\displaystyle -\log\sigma - \frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{z^2}{2}$ subtract: the $\tfrac12\log 2\pi$ terms cancel

Three terms. Next: average each one over $z \sim q$.

Gaussian KL, step 2 of 2

Average each term over the cloud $q$

$\E_q\big[(z-\mu)^2\big] = \sigma^2$ (that is the variance)
$\E_q\big[z^2\big] = \mu^2 + \sigma^2$ (mean squared + variance)
$$\KL = -\log\sigma - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{\mu^2+\sigma^2}{2} = \frac12\Big(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2\Big)$$
$$\mu = 1,\ \sigma = 0.5:\quad \tfrac12\big(1 + 0.25 - 1 + 1.386\big) = 0.818 \ \checkmark$$

Moving the mean away costs $\mu^2$; shrinking $\sigma$ costs $-\log\sigma^2$. For a code with several coordinates, add one such term per coordinate.

The check matches the shaded area from two slides ago. This exact formula is what the VAE code computes.

Why: KL(p‖q) is weighted by p, so q is punished wherever data exists and q is small. KL(q‖p) is weighted by q, so q is punished wherever it puts mass and the data is absent.

End of Part 3

What we did, and what comes next

What we did

  1. Surprise $= -\log p$. KL $=$ the average extra surprise from believing $Q$ when the truth is $P$.
  2. $\KL \ge 0$, zero only when the two match, and not symmetric.
  3. Cross-entropy $=$ entropy $+$ KL: training a classifier already minimises a KL.
  4. For Gaussians, per coordinate: $\KL = \tfrac12(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2)$.

Next: the loss itself

Part 1's catch: learning needs $p_\theta(x)$, an integral over every code. With the encoder and KL in hand, Part 4 turns that integral into a loss we can compute: the ELBO.
