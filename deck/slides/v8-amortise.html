Many data points: learn to predict $q$

So far: one coin, one $q$, 400 steps. A dataset has thousands of items, each with its own posterior. Running VI separately for each is wasteful. Learn a function from the data to $q$ instead.

A dataset of coins

{{vik.amort.n}} coins, each with its own hidden $z$

For each coin $n$: draw $z_n \sim \N(0, 1)$, toss it 10 times with $\theta_n = \mathrm{sigmoid}(z_n)$. The data: $x_n$, its ten tosses.

Each coin has its own posterior $p(z_n \mid x_n)$, and so its own best $q_n = \N(m_n, s_n^2)$.

Per-coin VI: {{vik.amort.n}} separate loops of 400 steps. A new coin: another loop.

Amortised inference

An encoder: a small network from $x$ to $q$'s two numbers

$x_n$10 tosses→ encoder10 → 16 → 2→ $m_n,\ \log s_n$→ $z = m_n + s_n\eps$→ $-\ELBO_n$ 
m, log_s = encoder(X).unbind(-1)                    # X: [500, 10] tosses, one row per coin
z   = m + log_s.exp() * torch.randn(500)          # one draw per coin
rec = h * F.logsigmoid(z) + (10 - h) * F.logsigmoid(-z)   # h: heads per coin
kl  = 0.5 * (m**2 + (2*log_s).exp() - 1 - 2*log_s)
loss = -(rec - kl).mean()                              # minus the average ELBO

Train the encoder's weights on the average ELBO over all coins: {{vik.amort.steps}} Adam steps, one draw per coin. Then one forward pass gives any coin's $q$.

Every line is something from today: the reparameterised draw (Part 6), the likelihood term (Part 2's log joint without the prior), the Gaussian KL to the prior (Part 4), the ELBO (Part 5). The only new thing is that m and log s come out of a network.

Does it work?

One pass of the encoder ≈ 400 steps of VI per coin

Black: per-coin VI, centre $m$ with $\pm s$. Purple: the encoder's $m$ for each of the {{vik.amort.n}} coins.

For the {{vik.amort.counts.8}} coins with 8 heads: VI gives $m = {{vik.amort.h8_m}}$, the encoder {{vik.amort.h8_enc_m}}; both give $s = {{vik.amort.h8_s}}$.

ELBO lost per coin, on average: {{vik.amort.gap_mean}} nats (at most {{vik.amort.gap_max}}). That loss is the amortisation gap.

That network is the VAE's encoder. The VAE also learns the decoder (here sigmoid$(z)$ was fixed), on the same ELBO.

"Amortised": the cost of inference is paid once, during training, and then spread over every future data point, like paying off a loan. The gap is tiny here because the map from heads to (m, s) is smooth and the encoder has plenty of capacity.

Before you leave

Five questions

  1. Why can't we compute $p(z\mid x)$ for the one-number decoder? The evidence $\int p(z)\,p(x\mid z)\,dz$ has no formula. A grid works in 1-D but explodes with dimension.
  2. $\log p(x) = \ELBO(q) + \,?$ $\KL(q\,\|\,p(z\mid x)) \ge 0$. So the ELBO is a lower bound, and since $\log p(x)$ does not depend on $q$, raising the ELBO shrinks the KL.
  3. Why does VI minimise $\KL(q\|p)$, not $\KL(p\|q)$? KL averages under its first argument: we can draw from $q$, not from the posterior. The price: $q$ is mode-seeking and often too narrow.
  4. What does reparameterisation buy? Gradients through random draws, with far less noise than the score function: {{vik.grad.ratio}}× fewer draws on the toy, {{vik.grad.coin_ratio}}× on the coin.
  5. A diagonal Gaussian $q$ fitted to a correlated target comes out too ...? Narrow: width {{vik.corr.mf_sd}} instead of 1 for $\rho = 0.9$.
