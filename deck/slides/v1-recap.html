Bayes' rule for machine learning

"Job" becomes the parameters $\theta$; "description" becomes the data $D$

$$\underbrace{p(\theta\mid D)}_{\text{posterior}} \;=\; \frac{\overbrace{p(D\mid\theta)}^{\text{likelihood}}\ \overbrace{p(\theta)}^{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{p(D)}_{\text{evidence}}}$$
$$p(D) = \int p(D\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)\,d\theta$$
$\theta$: a coin's chance of heads; a regression line's two weights; a network's millions of weights.
$D$: the tosses we saw; the $(x, y)$ points; the training set.
The posterior says which $\theta$'s explain $D$, and how sure we are.

For Steve the evidence was a sum over two jobs. Here it is an integral over every possible $\theta$.

PML Lecture 2, pp. 19–20 · PML Lecture 23, "Bayesian ML: Recap"

PML Lecture 23 (VI part 1) opens with this frame, at 0:00. The evidence does not depend on θ: it is the number that makes the posterior integrate to one.

The posterior is a distribution, not a single answer. Predictions average over it, which is where uncertainty comes from.

The example we will come back to all lecture ▶ N1

A coin: prior $\mathrm{Beta}(5, 3)$, then 17 heads in 30 tosses

Prior $\mathrm{Beta}(5, 3)$: before any tosses we think heads is a bit likelier (mean {{vik.coin.prior_mean}}).

Likelihood $\theta^{17}(1-\theta)^{13}$: the data alone peak at $17/30 = {{vik.coin.mle}}$.

Posterior $\propto$ prior $\times$ likelihood:

$$\theta^{4}(1-\theta)^{2}\cdot\theta^{17}(1-\theta)^{13} = \theta^{21}(1-\theta)^{15}$$

which is $\mathrm{Beta}(22, 16)$, mean {{vik.coin.post_mean}}.

The posterior is a compromise between the prior and the data, and it is narrower than both.

The denominator, for the coin ▶ N1

The coin's evidence has a formula, but only because we were lucky

$$p(D) = \int_0^1 \underbrace{\theta^{17}(1-\theta)^{13}}_{\text{likelihood}}\ \underbrace{\frac{\theta^{4}(1-\theta)^{2}}{B(5, 3)}}_{\text{prior}}\,d\theta \;=\; \frac{B(22, 16)}{B(5, 3)}$$
$\log p(D) = {{vik.coin.log_ev}}$from the formula; a 1-D grid over $\theta$ agrees: {{vik.coin.grid}}
$B(a, b) = \int_0^1 \theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1}d\theta$ is the Beta function. The product of prior and likelihood is again a Beta shape, so the integral is known. A prior with this property is called conjugate.

Remember $-21.40$: at the end of the lecture, variational inference will recover it without the formula.

p(D) is the probability of this particular sequence of 30 tosses, averaged over the prior, so it is tiny; we always work with its log.

Notebook N1: coin.log_evidence uses torch.lgamma; vu.evidence_grid integrates the joint on a fine grid with log-sum-exp. Both give −21.397.

PML: the Beta-Bernoulli "evidence" frames (MAP and Laplace lectures). The VI notebook's last section checks its answer against exactly this Beta(22, 16).

The denominator, for linear regression ▶ N1

Linear regression also has a formula for the evidence

100 points from $y = 1.5 + 0.5\,x + \text{noise}$ (noise sd 0.2). Prior on the two weights: $\theta \sim \N(0, I)$.

The evidence averages the likelihood over every line the prior allows (60 of them drawn, in orange):

$$p(D) = \int \N\big(\mathbf y \mid X\theta,\ \sigma^2 I\big)\,\N(\theta \mid 0, I)\,d\theta$$

Gaussian prior, Gaussian noise: the answer is again Gaussian,

$$p(D) = \N\big(\mathbf y \mid 0,\ XX^\top + \sigma^2 I\big), \quad \log p(D) = {{vik.linreg_ev.exact}}$$

Conjugate again. Now pretend we did not know the formula: could we estimate the integral by sampling the prior?

From PML Sampling-1 ("marginal likelihood" frames) and the notebook mc-linreg-evidence.ipynb, cells 19–22. In general p(D) = N(y | X m₀, X S₀ Xᵀ + σ²I); here m₀ = 0 and S₀ = I.

The log evidence is positive because these are densities over y with a small noise scale (0.2), not probabilities.

Notice how few of the orange lines go anywhere near the data. That is the next slide.

PML Sampling-1, "Log-sum-exp" and "Sampling converges slowly"; mc-linreg-evidence cells 14–18. The average of likelihoods is computed as logsumexp(log-likelihoods) − log N, because the likelihoods themselves underflow.

The worst draw here has a log-likelihood of {{vik.linreg_ev.worst}}. The estimate depends on the rare draws near the posterior, so it is noisy and biased low on the log scale: 11.20 in the PML run, 12.22 in ours, against 12.35.

The lesson for VI: draw θ from somewhere near the posterior, not from the prior. That "somewhere" will be q.

When there is no formula at all

Change the prior a little, and the integral has no closed form

The same coin, but put a Normal prior on its logit: $\theta = \mathrm{sigmoid}(w)$, $w \sim \N(0, 1)$.

$$p(D) = \int \N(w\mid 0, 1)\ \theta(w)^{17}\big(1-\theta(w)\big)^{13}\,dw$$

Bernoulli likelihood, Normal prior: not conjugate, no formula. In 1-D a grid still works ($\log p(D) = {{vik.logit.grid}}$).

A neural network has millions of weights: a grid with 100 points per weight needs $100^{\text{millions}}$ points. The evidence is intractable.

So we cannot normalise the posterior. We can still evaluate prior $\times$ likelihood at any $\theta$ we like.

PML Lecture 2, p. 77 ("Bernoulli + Normal: intractable") · PML Laplace notebook, "Normal prior for coin toss" · PML Lecture 23 at 3:06

The shaded curve is the joint p(D, w) = p(D | w) p(w) as a function of w. Its area is p(D). We can compute its height anywhere, but not its area by formula.

Notebook N1 also estimates this one by 100,000 prior draws: {{vik.logit.mc}}, close to the grid, because in 1-D the prior is not far from the posterior.

The key sentence for the rest of the lecture: we can evaluate the unnormalised posterior p(D | θ) p(θ), but not its normaliser p(D).

Three ways around it, on one hard shape ▶ N1

Laplace, MCMC and VI on a two-bump posterior

Laplace: a Gaussian at the highest peak, with its curvature: $\N({{vik.three.lap_m}},\ {{vik.three.lap_s}}^2)$. Ignores the second bump.
MCMC: a random walk whose visits follow the posterior. Mean {{vik.three.mcmc_mean}} (true {{vik.three.true_mean}}). Exact in the limit; slow.
VI, today: the closest Gaussian, found by optimisation: $\N({{vik.three.vi_m}},\ {{vik.three.vi_s}}^2)$. Fast; approximate.

"Closest" needs a measure of distance between two distributions. That is the KL divergence, Part 2.

The "posterior" here is the mixture 0.7 N(−2, 1²) + 0.3 N(2, 1²), the target of the PML Laplace notebook. The MCMC panel is random-walk Metropolis, 20,000 steps, acceptance {{vik.three.mcmc_acc}} (the PML course's hamiltorch GIF shows the same idea on a 2-D mixture).

This is the one slide on Laplace and MCMC; students met both in PML Lectures 8 and 13–14. VI turns inference into optimisation, which is what lets it scale to neural networks and the VAE.

The VI fit is broad here because the two bumps overlap. Part 2 shows what happens when they do not.

The variational idea, and today's map

Pick a simple family $q_\psi$, and find its member closest to the posterior

$$\psi^\star = \arg\min_{\psi}\ \KL\big(\,q_\psi(\theta)\ \|\ p(\theta\mid D)\,\big)$$
