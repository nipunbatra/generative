Products of many small numbers are awkward

Surprise $= -\log p$, and why the log

Which is more surprising: snow in Kashmir in January, or snow in Gandhinagar? What should "surprise" do?

1 · a certain event: no surprise. $-\log 1 = 0$.

2 · rarer means more surprising. $-\log p$ grows as $p$ shrinks.

3 · two independent digits: probabilities multiply, surprises should add. Only the log turns products into sums.

So the betting-game product becomes a sum of surprises, one per digit. Natural log, so the unit is the nat.

The Kashmir/Gandhinagar question is from the PML course's information-theory lecture. Following Artem Kirsanov's argument: the additivity requirement forces the logarithm. The base only changes the unit (bits for log base 2).

This is the same −log p as in every log-likelihood: the log-likelihood of a data set is minus its total surprise.

Average surprise, when you believe the truth

Entropy: $H(P) = 0.500$ nats per digit

$$H(P) = \sum_i P_i\,(-\log P_i) = \underbrace{0.8}_{\text{how often a 1}}\times 0.223 + \underbrace{0.2}_{\text{how often a 7}}\times 1.609 = 0.500$$

The weights are how often each digit really appears: eight in ten, two in ten. In Part 3's language, an average under $P$: $H(P) = \E_{x\sim P}[-\log P(x)]$.

Even the perfect model is surprised by 0.500 nats per digit on average: the data are random.

Same data, a different scorer

Cross-entropy: $H(P, Q) = 0.592$ nats per digit

$$H(P, Q) = \sum_i \cx{P_i}\,(-\log \cdec{Q_i}) = 0.8 \times 0.511 + 0.2 \times 0.916 = 0.592$$

Still weighted by $P$

the digits still come from the real data: eight 1s, two 7s.

Scored by $Q$

each digit's surprise now uses the model's probability.

Believing the wrong model costs more surprise on the same data: 0.592 instead of 0.500.

Kirsanov's rigged coin: you only get unusually surprised by ten heads in a row because you wrongly believe the coin is fair. The data did not change; your model did.

The extra surprise

KL divergence = cross-entropy − entropy

$H(P) = 0.500$unavoidable: the data are random
$0.092$KL

together: $H(P, Q) = 0.592$ (more precisely $0.5004 + 0.0915 = 0.5919$)

$$\KL(P\,\|\,Q) = H(P, Q) - H(P) = \sum_i P_i \log\frac{P_i}{Q_i} = 0.092 \text{ nats}$$

KL is the part of the surprise that comes only from using the wrong model. And it is the betting game's gap: $\tfrac{1}{10}\log 2.5 = 0.092$.

Digit by digit: a 1 contributes 0.8 × log(0.8/0.6) = 0.2301, a 7 contributes 0.2 × log(0.2/0.4) = −0.1386. One row is negative (Q over-predicts 7s, so a 7 surprises it less), but the total, 0.0915, is positive.

The game: (1/10) log(0.0067 / 0.0027) = (1/10) log 2.497 = 0.0915. KL is the log of the betting ratio, per data point.

Part 3's tool, already useful

KL is an average, so samples can estimate it

$$\KL(P\,\|\,Q) = \E_{x\sim P}\!\left[\log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\right] \approx \frac1S\sum_{s=1}^{S} \log\frac{P(x_s)}{Q(x_s)},\quad x_s\sim P$$
five draws: 0.0921, 1, 7, 1, 1 gives $\tfrac15\big(4\times 0.288 - 0.693\big)$, exactly 80/20 by luck
1,000 draws: {{vik.mc.kl1000}}{{vik.mc.kl1000_ones}} ones and the rest sevens, drawn at random

Notice what this needs: draws from the first argument, and both probabilities at each draw. Remember that for VI.

For two outcomes you would never estimate KL by sampling; you would just add two terms. For two distributions over 784 pixels, or over a code with no formula for its posterior, sampling is the only option.

Why KL can never be negative

One picture: $\log r$ lies under its tangent

Let $r = Q_i / P_i$. The curve $\log r$ never rises above the line $r - 1$.

$$\KL = \sum_i P_i\,(-\log r_i) \;\ge\; \sum_i P_i\,(1 - r_i)$$
$$= \sum_i P_i - \sum_i Q_i = 1 - 1 = 0$$

Equality only when every $r_i = 1$: $Q = P$. This one inequality is what makes the ELBO a lower bound in Part 5.

The tangent picture is from Jia-Bin Huang's KL video. Red segments: our two digits, r = 0.75 for a 1 and r = 2 for a 7. The gaps between the line and the curve are what makes KL positive. The same argument works for densities, with integrals instead of sums.

What the number means

KL is how fast evidence piles up

$$\frac{p(\text{data}\mid P)}{p(\text{data}\mid Q)} \approx e^{\,n\,\KL(P\|Q)}$$

After 10 digits the truth is {{vik.kl.r10}}× more likely; after 50, {{vik.kl.r50}}×; after 100, {{vik.kl.r100}}×.

A small KL means the two models are hard to tell apart from data; a large one means a few samples settle it.

The curve is exp(n × 0.0915): the typical betting ratio after n digits. With finite samples the real ratio fluctuates around it.

The order matters, sharply

A model that "forgets" 7s

Truth $P = (0.8, 0.2)$. A model $Q' = (0.99, 0.01)$ that almost never expects a 7.

$\KL(P\|Q') = {{vik.kl.forget_pq}}$real 7s keep showing up, and each costs $\log(0.2/0.01) = {{vik.kl.forget_cost}}$ nats
$\KL(Q'\|P) = {{vik.kl.forget_qp}}$averaged under $Q'$, which almost never draws a 7, so the costly digit rarely counts

Whoever sits first does the weighting. $\KL(P\|Q)$ punishes $Q$ for missing what $P$ does; $\KL(Q\|P)$ barely notices.

Hold on to "whoever sits first does the weighting": it explains mode-covering vs mode-seeking at the end of this part, and why VI's q comes out too narrow in Part 7.

Why training code says "cross-entropy"

Cross-entropy = entropy + KL

$H(P,Q)$$=$$\displaystyle -\sum_i P_i\log Q_i$ cross-entropy: the loss you train classifiers with $=$$\displaystyle -\sum_i P_i\log P_i \;+\; \sum_i P_i\big(\log P_i - \log Q_i\big)$ add and subtract $\sum_i P_i\log P_i$ $=$$\displaystyle \underbrace{H(P)}_{\text{entropy: fixed by the data}} \;+\; \KL(P\,\|\,Q)$

Lowering cross-entropy can only lower the KL part. So maximum likelihood minimises $\KL(\text{data}\,\|\,\text{model})$: data first.

0.592 = 0.500 + 0.092. Every generative model trained by maximum likelihood (Gaussian mixtures, autoregressive models, the decoder half of a VAE) is minimising this forward KL. Variational inference will use the other order.

Why this pair: p = 𝒩(0, 1) is the VAE's prior. The next two slides get the formula for KL(q‖p), which is exactly the VAE's KL term.

The formula, step 1 of 2

Write out the log of the ratio

$\log q(z)$$=$$\displaystyle -\log\sigma - \tfrac12\log 2\pi - \frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$ log of $\N(z;\mu,\sigma^2)$ $\log p(z)$$=$$\displaystyle -\tfrac12\log 2\pi - \frac{z^2}{2}$ log of $\N(z;0,1)$ $\log\dfrac{q(z)}{p(z)}$$=$$\displaystyle -\log\sigma - \frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{z^2}{2}$ subtract: the $\tfrac12\log 2\pi$ terms cancel

Three terms. Next: average each one over $z \sim q$.

The formula, step 2 of 2

Average each term over $q$

$\E_q\big[(z-\mu)^2\big] = \sigma^2$ (that is the variance)
$\E_q\big[z^2\big] = \mu^2 + \sigma^2$ (Part 3's example)
$$\KL = -\log\sigma - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{\mu^2+\sigma^2}{2} = \frac12\Big(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2\Big)$$
$$\mu = 1,\ \sigma = 0.5:\quad \tfrac12\big(1 + 0.25 - 1 + 1.386\big) = 0.818 \ \checkmark$$

Moving the centre costs $\tfrac12\mu^2$; a width other than 1 costs $\tfrac12(\sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2)$. This is the KL term of every VAE.

The check matches the shaded area on the widget (computed numerically there: {{vik.kl.gauss_num}}). With several independent coordinates, add one such term per coordinate.

For two general Gaussians (the exercise in the PML course's VI slides): KL(𝒩(μq, σq²) ‖ 𝒩(μp, σp²)) = ½[log(σp²/σq²) − 1 + (μq − μp)²/σp² + σq²/σp²]. Setting μp = 0 and σp = 1 gives the formula here.

Why the two directions behave so differently

Whoever sits first does the weighting

$\KL(p\|q) = \E_{p}\big[\log p - \log q\big]$

Averaged where the data are. Anywhere $p$ has mass and $q$ is tiny, $-\log q$ is huge. So $q$ stretches to cover all of $p$, even the empty middle.

$\KL(q\|p) = \E_{q}\big[\log q - \log p\big]$

Averaged where $q$ puts mass. Anywhere $q$ has mass and $p$ is tiny, $-\log p$ is huge. So $q$ avoids the gaps and hides inside one bump.

Variational inference will minimise $\KL(q\,\|\,\text{posterior})$: the second kind. Expect $q$ to be cautious, never too wide, often too narrow.

Maximum likelihood (previous slides) is the first kind: that is why a single Gaussian fitted by maximum likelihood to two bumps sits in the empty middle. VI is the second kind, forced on us by Part 3's catch: we can only average under a distribution we can sample.
