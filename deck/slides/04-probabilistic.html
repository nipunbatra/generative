Maximum likelihood for our 3

How probable is our 3 under the model?

With the coin we summed over two values:

$$p(x) = \sum_{z\in\{\mathrm A,\mathrm B\}} p(z)\,p(x\mid z)$$

A code can be any point in the plane, so the sum becomes an integral:

$$p_\theta(x) = \int \cz{p(z)}\;\cdec{p_\theta(x\mid z)}\,dz$$

2 numbers per code, so we can afford a 901 × 901 grid of decoder calls: $\log p_\theta(x) = {{evidence.log_px}}$ nats.

The map shows log p(x | z) for our 3 at every code z: dark = codes that decode to something like our 3. Dashed circles: 1, 2, 3 prior standard deviations.

Real computation: 811,801 decoder calls, then log-sum-exp. The decoder behind every number in Parts 3 and 5 is the 2-number one trained in Part 7; here it is simply "a trained decoder".

Why not just compute $p_\theta(x)$?

The integral is intractable

No formula. With two bumps, $p(x)$ was a sum of 2 terms. Here it is $\int p(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)\,dz$ with a neural network inside: no algebra gives the answer.

Too many terms to add up. Put a grid of 901 points on every axis of the code and add up the decoder's answers:

"Intractable" means exactly this: no formula, and far too many terms to sum.

Idea: estimate the integral by sampling

Random codes almost never hit the right patch

$$p_\theta(x) = \underbrace{\E_{z\sim p(z)}\big[\,p_\theta(x\mid z)\,\big]}_{\text{the average over codes from the prior}} \approx \frac1S\sum_{s=1}^{S} p_\theta(x\mid z_s)$$
100,000random codes $z_s\sim\N(0,I)$

{{evidence.llik_best}}the best of the 100,000: $\log p(x\mid z)$

A typical random code makes our 3 about $e^{243}$ times less probable than the best code. Blind sampling wastes almost every draw.

The estimate is unbiased but its variance is enormous: the average is dominated by the rare draws that hit the patch, and with 16 or 100 dimensions you would essentially never hit it.

Better: know where the patch is before sampling. That is what the rest of this part, and Part 5, are about. 𝔼 means "average over"; the VI lecture (its Part 2) writes KL as one.

Inference, like the coin and the bumps

The backwards step: which codes could have made our 3?

Part 1's Bayes table, with a plane of codes instead of two bumps:

$$p_\theta(z\mid x) = \frac{p(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)}{\hl{p_\theta(x)}}$$

The bottom is the evidence: the same impossible integral. So the posterior is out of reach too.

From the 2-number grid we can still look at it: a small, slightly tilted patch near $({{vi.mode.0}},\ {{vi.mode.1}})$, about 0.05 wide. The prior is 1 wide: one image pins its code down tightly.

Training needs this patch for every image. Next: three ways to approximate a posterior we cannot compute.

Zoomed view around our 3; shaded: the true posterior, normalised on the fine grid. Only possible because this code has 2 numbers.

Why training needs the posterior: to improve the decoder for our 3, we need to know which codes our 3 came from, exactly as the two-bump fit needed p(bump | x).

When the posterior cannot be computed, approximate it

Three ways, all run on our 3's real posterior

Laplace: find the peak, fit a Gaussian to the curvature there. Fast, local.

MCMC: a random walk that visits regions in proportion to the posterior. Exact in the long run, slow.

Variational: choose a simple family $q$ and optimise it to be as close as possible. Fast, trainable.

Today: only the variational one. It turns inference into optimisation, which is what neural networks are good at.

All three computed for these slides on the trained 2-number VAE decoder, for test digit #90. Shaded: the true posterior on a fine grid. Laplace: peak at ({{vi.mode.0}}, {{vi.mode.1}}), standard deviations {{vi.lap_sd.0}} and {{vi.lap_sd.1}}. MCMC: random-walk Metropolis, {{vi.mcmc_n}} kept samples, acceptance {{vi.mcmc_acc}}. Variational: diagonal Gaussian, 800 gradient steps.

Students have met these in the probabilistic machine learning course; here they only need the idea of each and why we pick VI.

The variational idea, for our 3

Propose a simple $q$, then pull it towards the true posterior

$$q(z) = \N\big(m,\ \mathrm{diag}(s_1^2, s_2^2)\big)$$

Four numbers to choose: a centre $m = (m_1, m_2)$ and two widths $s_1, s_2$.

Goal: make $q(z)$ as close as possible to $p_\theta(z\mid x)$, the shaded patch.

"As close as possible" needs a number for how far apart two distributions are.

That number is the KL divergence: the first thing in Session 2.

The purple ellipses (one and two standard deviations) are the q that variational inference finds in Part 5: centre ({{vi.mu.0}}, {{vi.mu.1}}), widths {{vi.sigma.0}} and {{vi.sigma.1}}. A diagonal Gaussian cannot tilt, so it can match the patch closely but not exactly.

What we did, and what comes next

What we did

  1. Learn = make the data probable; generate = sample. Code: one loop.
  2. Autoencoders compress and rebuild (plain, denoising, sparse, contractive), but cannot generate.
  3. As a probability model: prior $p(z) = \N(0, I)$, likelihood $p_\theta(x\mid z)$ from the decoder.
  4. Training needs $p_\theta(x)$ and the posterior $p_\theta(z\mid x)$: both intractable.
  5. Variational idea: a simple $q$, pulled close to the posterior.
