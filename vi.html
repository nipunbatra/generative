The data

Ten tosses: 8 heads, 2 tails

HHTHHHHTHH

What is this coin's chance of heads? Call it $\theta$.

The obvious answer: $8/10 = 0.8$. That is the maximum-likelihood estimate.

But ten tosses do not rule out $\theta = 0.7$, or even $0.5$. We want a whole distribution over $\theta$: how strongly we believe each value.

Bayes' rule turns "what I believed before" and "what I saw" into "what I believe now".

Call the ten tosses x. Order does not matter for the maths, but keep the sequence on screen: every number in the next slides is about exactly this sequence.

Ask: would you bet even money that the coin is fair? Most say no. Would you bet your house that θ is exactly 0.8? Also no. That in-between state of belief is what a posterior is.

Add up the joint column: $25.63\times10^{-4}$. Keep this number; it has a name.

Posterior = joint ÷ sum. The coin with $\theta = 0.8$ now has 87% of our belief. The sum we divided by is $p(x)$, the evidence.

Likelihood of the 0.8 coin: 0.8^8 × 0.2^2 = 0.16777 × 0.04 = 0.00671. (The same product appears in the KL betting game later, by design.) The 0.5 coin: 0.5^10 = 0.000977. The 0.2 coin: 0.2^8 × 0.8^2 = 1.6 × 10⁻⁶.

Joint = likelihood / 3. Posterior: 22.37 / 25.63 = 0.873, 3.26 / 25.63 = 0.127, and 0.0002 for the 0.2 coin.

Point at the division: without it the joint column does not add up to 1. That is the only job of the evidence, and it is the thing that becomes impossible later.

The same table, in symbols

Bayes' rule, and the troublemaker at the bottom

$$\underbrace{\cx{p(\theta\mid x)}}_{\text{posterior}} \;=\; \frac{\overbrace{\cz{p(\theta)}}^{\text{prior}}\;\overbrace{\cdec{p(x\mid\theta)}}^{\text{likelihood}}}{\underbrace{p(x)}_{\text{evidence}}}$$
$$p(x) = \sum_{\theta\,\in\,\{0.2,\,0.5,\,0.8\}} p(\theta)\,p(x\mid\theta) \qquad \text{three coins: a sum of three terms}$$
$$p(x) = \int_0^1 p(\theta)\,p(x\mid\theta)\,d\theta \qquad \text{every } \theta \text{ between 0 and 1: an integral}$$

The evidence is the joint, added up over every possible value of the hidden quantity. Watch it: it is the whole difficulty.

Top: read it as "posterior ∝ prior × likelihood, then rescale". The evidence does not depend on θ: it is one number for the data set, the same in every row of the table.

The colours stay fixed for the whole lecture: orange = prior, teal = likelihood, blue = posterior (and later, purple = our approximation q).

Every value of $\theta$ at once

Multiply the two curves point by point, rescale to area 1. Posterior mean 0.714: between the prior's 0.5 and the data's 0.8.

The posterior's exponents just add: θ¹(1−θ)¹ from the prior times θ⁸(1−θ)² from the data gives θ⁹(1−θ)³, which is Beta(10, 4). Mean 10/14 = 0.714, mode 9/12 = 0.75, standard deviation 0.117.

Why the posterior stays a Beta: the prior and the likelihood have the same form, θ to a power times (1 − θ) to a power. That luck is called conjugacy, and it is what disappears in Part 2.

For this prior, we got lucky

The evidence has a formula

$$p(x) = \int_0^1 \underbrace{\cz{6\theta(1-\theta)}}_{\text{prior}}\;\underbrace{\cdec{\theta^8(1-\theta)^2}}_{\text{likelihood}}\,d\theta = 6\int_0^1 \theta^9(1-\theta)^3\,d\theta = 6\,B(10, 4)$$

$B(a, b)$, the Beta function, is a tabulated integral: $B(10, 4) = \dfrac{9!\;3!}{13!}$. So $p(x) = 0.00210$ and $\log p(x) = −6.167$.

The integrand is a power of $\theta$ times a power of $(1-\theta)$: the same form as the prior. That is conjugacy. It is rare, and it is the only reason this integral could be done.

Change the prior slightly and the luck runs out. That is Part 2.

Check: 9! 3! / 13! = 362,880 × 6 / 6,227,020,800 = 3.4965 × 10⁻⁴; times 6 is 0.0020979. In logs, −6.167 nats. Compare with the three-coin table's 25.63 × 10⁻⁴: different, because there the prior allowed only three coins.

Note that x here is the exact sequence of ten tosses, so there is no binomial coefficient.

Same coin, a different story for $\theta$

A one-number decoder

Draw a hidden number $z \sim \N(0, 1)$, squash it: $\theta = \mathrm{sigmoid}(z)$, toss ten times with that $\theta$.

The VAE's story in miniature: a prior on a code, then a network that turns the code into probabilities.

The prior it implies on $\theta$ is almost Part 1's $\mathrm{Beta}(2, 2)$: nearly the same beliefs.

The hidden quantity is now $z$, a real number. From here on, $z$ is what we infer.

Middle panel: sigmoid(z) = 1 / (1 + e^(−z)), the last layer of every binary classifier and of the MNIST VAE's decoder (one per pixel). Here there is one "pixel", the coin, observed ten times.

Right panel: if z ~ N(0, 1), then θ = sigmoid(z) has this distribution (the "logit-normal"). The dashed curve is Beta(2, 2). They are close, which is why the evidence will come out almost the same (−6.164 vs −6.167).

Bayes' rule, exactly as before

The posterior over $z$

$$\cx{p(z\mid x)} = \frac{\cz{\N(z; 0, 1)}\;\cdec{\mathrm{sigmoid}(z)^8\,\big(1-\mathrm{sigmoid}(z)\big)^2}}{\displaystyle\int \N(z; 0, 1)\;\mathrm{sigmoid}(z)^8\,\big(1-\mathrm{sigmoid}(z)\big)^2\,dz}$$

The top: easy

A bell curve times a few sigmoids. Pick any $z$ and it is one line of arithmetic.

The bottom: no formula

No table of integrals has this one. No trick turns it into a Beta function: the bell curve and the sigmoids do not combine.

"Intractable" means exactly this: we can write the integral down, but not work it out.

This is the same situation as the VAE, where the top is "prior × decoder" (easy, one forward pass) and the bottom integrates the decoder over every code.

"Non-conjugate": the prior (Gaussian in z) and the likelihood (sigmoids of z) have different forms, so the product is not a known distribution.

What we can compute, and what we cannot

Any point of the curve, but not its area

def log_joint(z):          # log p(x, z), any z
    return (Normal(0, 1).log_prob(z)
            + 8 * F.logsigmoid(z)
            + 2 * F.logsigmoid(-z))

The two dots: $\log p(x, z) = −7.85$ at $z = 0$ and $−6.552$ at $z = 1$.

The shaded area is $p(x)$. To get it we would have to add up the curve everywhere.

We can evaluate the joint at any point. We cannot get its area. Variational inference will need only the first.

The joint p(x, z) = p(z) p(x | z) is the unnormalised posterior: the posterior's shape, but with area p(x) instead of 1. log_joint(1.0) = −1.419 (the prior's log density at 1) − 5.133 (the log-likelihood at 1) = −6.552.

logsigmoid(−z) is log(1 − sigmoid(z)). Using logs keeps tiny probabilities from rounding to zero: the same reason every loss in deep learning works in logs.

In one dimension, there is a way out

Add up thin rectangles

12,001 values of $z$ from $-6$ to $6$, spacing $\Delta z = 0.001$ (26 drawn here):

$$p(x) \approx \sum_k p(x, z_k)\,\Delta z = 0.00210$$

$\log p(x) = −6.164$ nats.

Divide the curve by it, and we have the exact posterior: mean 0.94, standard deviation 0.586.

We keep this exact answer only to check every approximation in this lecture. No approximation will be allowed to use it.

The Beta prior gave log p(x) = −6.167; the one-number decoder gives −6.164: almost the same prior, almost the same evidence.

When the posterior cannot be computed, approximate it

Three ways, on the same posterior

Laplace: a Gaussian fitted to the curvature at the peak, $z = 0.9$. Fast; sees only the peak.

MCMC: a random walk that visits $z$ in proportion to the posterior. Exact in the long run; slow.

VI: the closest member of a simple family, $\N(0.94,\ 0.58^2)$. Inference becomes optimisation.

The rest of today is only the third. It needs two tools first: averages from samples, and a number for "close".

Laplace and MCMC were lectures 8 and 13 to 14 of the probabilistic ML course; one slide is enough here. Laplace: width 0.572, found by Newton's method to the mode, then sd = 1 / sqrt(curvature) = 1 / sqrt(1 + 10 θ(1 − θ)) at the mode. Its evidence estimate, −6.175, is close to the true −6.164 because this posterior is nearly Gaussian.

MCMC: random-walk Metropolis with step size 1, 6,000 steps of which the first 1,000 are burn-in (kept visits: mean 0.92, spread 0.59), acceptance rate 0.55. Each step needs only the joint (the one line of code), never p(x): the ratio of two posteriors cancels the evidence. The inset shows the chain wandering.

Why VI for deep learning: it produces a q with a formula (fast to sample, fast to evaluate), and its objective can be climbed by gradient ascent on a GPU, for millions of data points at once.

Notation for the rest of the lecture

$\E$ means "average over"

$$\E_{\theta\sim p(\theta\mid x)}[\theta] = 0.0002\times 0.2 + 0.127\times 0.5 + 0.873\times 0.8 = 0.762$$
the three-coin posterior (Part 1): the chance the next toss is heads
$$\E_{\theta\sim \mathrm{Beta}(10, 4)}[\theta] = \int_0^1 \theta\;p(\theta\mid x)\,d\theta = \tfrac{10}{14} = 0.714$$
a continuous variable: the sum becomes an integral
$$\E_{z\sim q}\big[f(z)\big] = \int q(z)\,f(z)\,dz$$
in general: weight each value $f(z)$ by how likely $z$ is under $q$

The subscript says which distribution we average under. It will matter: KL and the ELBO are both averages, and the choice of subscript is the key decision in VI.

Posterior predictive: a posterior is not just for looking at; averages under it answer questions. The three-coin answer 0.762 and the Beta answer 0.714 differ because the priors differ.

Read "z ∼ q" aloud as "z drawn from q".

The one idea we need: Monte Carlo

Replace the integral by an average of draws

$$\E_{z\sim q}\big[f(z)\big] \;\approx\; \frac1S\sum_{s=1}^{S} f(z_s), \qquad z_s \sim q$$

How noisy is the estimate?

The error shrinks like $1/\sqrt{S}$

One draw of $z^2$ has a spread (standard deviation) of 1.061.

An average of $S$ draws has spread $1.061/\sqrt{S}$: 0.335, 0.106, 0.034 for $S$ = 10, 100, 1,000.

Measured over 2,000 repeats: 0.341, 0.107, 0.033.

Centred on the right answer (unbiased), but noisy: 100 times more draws buy 10 times less noise.

For z ∼ N(μ, σ²), Var(z²) = 4μ²σ² + 2σ⁴ = 1 + 0.125, so the one-draw spread is √1.125 = 1.061. The average of S independent draws has variance Var/S.

Two facts to carry forward. Unbiased: on average the estimate is exactly right, which is all stochastic gradient descent needs. 1/√S: the cost of precision grows quickly, so in practice we use few draws (the VAE uses one per image).

In code

Three lines, in any number of dimensions

mu, sigma = 1.0, 0.5
z = mu + sigma * torch.randn(1000)   # 1,000 draws from q
estimate = (z**2).mean()             # about 1.25

Draw from $q$: standard noise, stretched by $\sigma$ and shifted by $\mu$. Remember this line: it returns in Part 6.

Average $f$: evaluate $f$ at each draw and take the mean. No integral anywhere.

The catch: we need draws from the distribution we average under. For a $q$ we chose, easy. For the posterior, that is the very problem we started with.

So variational inference will arrange for every average to be under $q$, never under the posterior.

With 100,000 draws this gives 1.25. The same three lines work for a 32-dimensional z: draw a 32-vector of noise per sample. That is why Monte Carlo, unlike the grid, does not explode with dimension.

Products of many small numbers are awkward

Surprise $= -\log p$, and why the log

Which is more surprising: snow in Kashmir in January, or snow in Gandhinagar? What should "surprise" do?

1 · a certain event: no surprise. $-\log 1 = 0$.

2 · rarer means more surprising. $-\log p$ grows as $p$ shrinks.

3 · two independent digits: probabilities multiply, surprises should add. Only the log turns products into sums.

So the betting-game product becomes a sum of surprises, one per digit. Natural log, so the unit is the nat.

The Kashmir/Gandhinagar question is from the PML course's information-theory lecture. Following Artem Kirsanov's argument: the additivity requirement forces the logarithm. The base only changes the unit (bits for log base 2).

This is the same −log p as in every log-likelihood: the log-likelihood of a data set is minus its total surprise.

Average surprise, when you believe the truth

Entropy: $H(P) = 0.500$ nats per digit

$$H(P) = \sum_i P_i\,(-\log P_i) = \underbrace{0.8}_{\text{how often a 1}}\times 0.223 + \underbrace{0.2}_{\text{how often a 7}}\times 1.609 = 0.500$$

The weights are how often each digit really appears: eight in ten, two in ten. In Part 3's language, an average under $P$: $H(P) = \E_{x\sim P}[-\log P(x)]$.

Even the perfect model is surprised by 0.500 nats per digit on average: the data are random.

Same data, a different scorer

Cross-entropy: $H(P, Q) = 0.592$ nats per digit

$$H(P, Q) = \sum_i \cx{P_i}\,(-\log \cdec{Q_i}) = 0.8 \times 0.511 + 0.2 \times 0.916 = 0.592$$

Still weighted by $P$

the digits still come from the real data: eight 1s, two 7s.

Scored by $Q$

each digit's surprise now uses the model's probability.

Believing the wrong model costs more surprise on the same data: 0.592 instead of 0.500.

Kirsanov's rigged coin: you only get unusually surprised by ten heads in a row because you wrongly believe the coin is fair. The data did not change; your model did.

The extra surprise

together: $H(P, Q) = 0.592$ (more precisely $0.5004 + 0.0915 = 0.5919$)

KL is the part of the surprise that comes only from using the wrong model. And it is the betting game's gap: $\tfrac{1}{10}\log 2.5 = 0.092$.

Digit by digit: a 1 contributes 0.8 × log(0.8/0.6) = 0.2301, a 7 contributes 0.2 × log(0.2/0.4) = −0.1386. One row is negative (Q over-predicts 7s, so a 7 surprises it less), but the total, 0.0915, is positive.

The game: (1/10) log(0.0067 / 0.0027) = (1/10) log 2.497 = 0.0915. KL is the log of the betting ratio, per data point.

$$\KL(P\,\|\,Q) = \E_{x\sim P}\!\left[\log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\right] \approx \frac1S\sum_{s=1}^{S} \log\frac{P(x_s)}{Q(x_s)},\quad x_s\sim P$$
five draws: 0.0921, 1, 7, 1, 1 gives $\tfrac15\big(4\times 0.288 - 0.693\big)$, exactly 80/20 by luck
1,000 draws: 0.093802 ones and the rest sevens, drawn at random

Notice what this needs: draws from the first argument, and both probabilities at each draw. Remember that for VI.

For two outcomes you would never estimate KL by sampling; you would just add two terms. For two distributions over 784 pixels, or over a code with no formula for its posterior, sampling is the only option.

Why KL can never be negative

One picture: $\log r$ lies under its tangent

Let $r = Q_i / P_i$. The curve $\log r$ never rises above the line $r - 1$.

$$\KL = \sum_i P_i\,(-\log r_i) \;\ge\; \sum_i P_i\,(1 - r_i)$$
$$= \sum_i P_i - \sum_i Q_i = 1 - 1 = 0$$

Equality only when every $r_i = 1$: $Q = P$. This one inequality is what makes the ELBO a lower bound in Part 5.

The tangent picture is from Jia-Bin Huang's KL video. Red segments: our two digits, r = 0.75 for a 1 and r = 2 for a 7. The gaps between the line and the curve are what makes KL positive. The same argument works for densities, with integrals instead of sums.

What the number means

KL is how fast evidence piles up

$$\frac{p(\text{data}\mid P)}{p(\text{data}\mid Q)} \approx e^{\,n\,\KL(P\|Q)}$$

After 10 digits the truth is 2.5× more likely; after 50, 97×; after 100, 9,430×.

A small KL means the two models are hard to tell apart from data; a large one means a few samples settle it.

The curve is exp(n × 0.0915): the typical betting ratio after n digits. With finite samples the real ratio fluctuates around it.

Why training code says "cross-entropy"

Cross-entropy = entropy + KL

$H(P,Q)$$=$$\displaystyle -\sum_i P_i\log Q_i$ cross-entropy: the loss you train classifiers with $=$$\displaystyle -\sum_i P_i\log P_i \;+\; \sum_i P_i\big(\log P_i - \log Q_i\big)$ add and subtract $\sum_i P_i\log P_i$ $=$$\displaystyle \underbrace{H(P)}_{\text{entropy: fixed by the data}} \;+\; \KL(P\,\|\,Q)$

Lowering cross-entropy can only lower the KL part. So maximum likelihood minimises $\KL(\text{data}\,\|\,\text{model})$: data first.

0.592 = 0.500 + 0.092. Every generative model trained by maximum likelihood (Gaussian mixtures, autoregressive models, the decoder half of a VAE) is minimising this forward KL. Variational inference will use the other order.

Why the two directions behave so differently

Whoever sits first does the weighting

$\KL(p\|q) = \E_{p}\big[\log p - \log q\big]$

Averaged where the data are. Anywhere $p$ has mass and $q$ is tiny, $-\log q$ is huge. So $q$ stretches to cover all of $p$, even the empty middle.

$\KL(q\|p) = \E_{q}\big[\log q - \log p\big]$

Averaged where $q$ puts mass. Anywhere $q$ has mass and $p$ is tiny, $-\log p$ is huge. So $q$ avoids the gaps and hides inside one bump.

Variational inference will minimise $\KL(q\,\|\,\text{posterior})$: the second kind. Expect $q$ to be cautious, never too wide, often too narrow.

Maximum likelihood (previous slides) is the first kind: that is why a single Gaussian fitted by maximum likelihood to two bumps sits in the empty middle. VI is the second kind, forced on us by Part 3's catch: we can only average under a distribution we can sample.

Step 2: the gradient. The subtle part.

The gradient of an average whose draws move

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial m}\;\E_{z\sim\N(m,\,s^2)}\big[f(z)\big] \;=\; ?$$

Move $m$ and the draws themselves move. The randomness depends on what we differentiate.

A toy with a known answer (Part 3's): $q = \N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $\mu = 1$, $\sigma = 0.5$, and $f(z) = z^2$. Since $\E[z^2] = \mu^2 + \sigma^2$, the exact gradient is $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\mu}(\mu^2 + \sigma^2) = 2\mu = 2$.

Two standard ways to estimate it from draws: the score function, and reparameterisation.

We write μ, σ for the toy and m, s for the coin's q; they play the same role. The VAE lecture uses μ, σ for the encoder's outputs.

Way 1

The score function (the log-derivative trick)

$\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\displaystyle\int q_\mu(z)\,f(z)\,dz$$=$$\displaystyle\int \frac{\partial q_\mu(z)}{\partial\mu}\,f(z)\,dz$ $f$ does not depend on $\mu$; only $q$ does $=$$\displaystyle\int q_\mu(z)\,\frac{\partial \log q_\mu(z)}{\partial\mu}\,f(z)\,dz$ $\partial q = q\,\partial\log q$: multiply and divide by $q$ $=$$\displaystyle\E_{z\sim q}\Big[f(z)\,\frac{z-\mu}{\sigma^2}\Big]$ an average under $q$ again; for a Gaussian, $\partial\log q/\partial\mu = (z-\mu)/\sigma^2$

Estimate it with draws: average $f(z)\,(z-\mu)/\sigma^2$. Works for any $q$ and any $f$, even an $f$ you cannot differentiate.

Also called REINFORCE (from reinforcement learning, where f is a reward you cannot differentiate). ∂ log q / ∂μ is called the score of q.

Way 2

Reparameterisation: stretch and shift standard noise

$$z = \mu + \sigma\eps,\ \eps\sim\N(0,1) \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \E_{z\sim q}\big[f(z)\big] = \E_{\eps}\big[f(\mu + \sigma\eps)\big] \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\E = \E_{\eps}\big[f'(\mu + \sigma\eps)\big]$$

Now the randomness, $\eps$, does not depend on $\mu$. The parameter sits inside $f$'s argument, and the ordinary chain rule applies.

Follow the red dot: ε = −0.4, scaled by σ = 0.5 to −0.2, shifted by μ = 1 to 0.8. Same distribution as before; the draw is now a deterministic function of (μ, σ, ε).

Part 3's sampling line, z = mu + sigma * torch.randn(...), was already reparameterised. That is why it matters how you write a draw.

Variational inference for the coin

The whole loop: two numbers, gradient ascent on the ELBO

def neg_elbo(q, S=10):
    z = q.rsample((S,))                       # S reparameterised draws
    return -(log_joint(z) - q.log_prob(z)).mean()

m     = torch.tensor(0.0, requires_grad=True)   # start at the prior
log_s = torch.tensor(0.0, requires_grad=True)   # s = exp(log_s) = 1
opt = torch.optim.Adam([m, log_s], lr=0.05)

for step in range(400):
    loss = neg_elbo(Normal(m, log_s.exp()))
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

A tilted target, a $q$ that cannot tilt

Mean-field $q$ on a correlated Gaussian: too narrow

$$q(z_1, z_2) = \N(z_1;\,m_1, s_1^2)\;\N(z_2;\,m_2, s_2^2)$$

One width per coordinate, independent under $q$: mean-field. Target: correlation $\rho = 0.9$, each coordinate's spread 1.

Left, VI's direction: $q$ must not put mass where $p$ is thin, so it shrinks inside the ellipse. Width $\sqrt{1-\rho^2} = 0.436$, not 1.

Right, the other direction: wide enough to cover, width 1, but lots of mass where $p$ has none.

Mean-field VI underestimates uncertainty. Both fits pay the same KL here, 0.830 nats, by opposite mistakes.

This is Bishop, PRML, Figure 10.2. Blue: the target. Purple: q's 1- and 2-standard-deviation ellipses. Closed form: the mean-field optimum of reverse KL has precision equal to the target's diagonal precision, 1/(1 − ρ²), so variance 1 − ρ² = 0.19.

Where tilted posteriors come from: Bayesian linear regression's intercept and slope (the PML course's last VI example), or two pixels of a code that trade off against each other.

The coincidence of equal KLs: both are −½ log(1 − ρ²) = 0.830 for this target.

Give $q$ a tilt

A full-covariance $q$ fits the tilted target exactly

$$z = m + L\eps,\quad L = \begin{pmatrix} \ell_{11} & 0 \\ \ell_{21} & \ell_{22}\end{pmatrix},\quad \eps\sim\N(0, I)$$

Reparameterisation in 2-D: one extra number, $\ell_{21}$, lets $q$ tilt.

Both fitted by the same loop (reverse KL, rsample, Adam). Diagonal: KL 0.828. Full: KL 0.000, correlation 0.90.

With $d$ numbers in $z$: $d$ widths for a diagonal $q$, $d(d+1)/2$ for a full one. The VAE keeps diagonal: cheaper, and its decoder adapts.

L is the Cholesky factor: q's covariance is L Lᵀ. The two diagonal entries are learned as logs (positive widths); ℓ21 can be any number. For 32 numbers, a full covariance needs 528 numbers per data point instead of 32.

The fix: a richer $q$

Push simple noise through a curve: a flow

$z = f(\eps)$ with $\eps \sim \N(0, 1)$ and $f$ monotone. The right curve makes $q$ match the skewed target exactly.

Normalizing flows learn $f$ as an invertible neural network, so $\log q(z)$ stays computable. Reparameterisation still works: $z$ is a differentiable function of $\eps$.

Richer families (flows, mixtures) shrink the gap; the ELBO and the loop do not change at all.

Honest note: this f was not learned. It is the exact map that turns 𝒩(0, 1) into the target (the target's inverse CDF applied to the normal CDF), computed numerically to show what a flow aims for. A trained flow approximates such a map. Change of variables: log q(z) = log 𝒩(ε) − log |f′(ε)|.

Amortised inference

An encoder: a small network from $x$ to $q$'s two numbers

rec = h * F.logsigmoid(z) + (10 - h) * F.logsigmoid(-z)   # h: heads per coin
kl  = 0.5 * (m**2 + (2*log_s).exp() - 1 - 2*log_s)
loss = -(rec - kl).mean()                              # minus the average ELBO

Train the encoder's weights on the average ELBO over all coins: 3,000 Adam steps, one draw per coin. Then one forward pass gives any coin's $q$.

Before you leave

Five questions

  1. Why can't we compute $p(z\mid x)$ for the one-number decoder? The evidence $\int p(z)\,p(x\mid z)\,dz$ has no formula. A grid works in 1-D but explodes with dimension.
  2. $\log p(x) = \ELBO(q) + \,?$ $\KL(q\,\|\,p(z\mid x)) \ge 0$. So the ELBO is a lower bound, and since $\log p(x)$ does not depend on $q$, raising the ELBO shrinks the KL.
  3. Why does VI minimise $\KL(q\|p)$, not $\KL(p\|q)$? KL averages under its first argument: we can draw from $q$, not from the posterior. The price: $q$ is mode-seeking and often too narrow.
  4. What does reparameterisation buy? Gradients through random draws, with far less noise than the score function: 22× fewer draws on the toy, 13× on the coin.
  5. A diagonal Gaussian $q$ fitted to a correlated target comes out too ...? Narrow: width 0.436 instead of 1 for $\rho = 0.9$.
