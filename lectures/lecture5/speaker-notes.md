## gen-disc: Generative or discriminative, in one picture

**Build 1, ask:** Where is p(x) high? Where is it near 0?

**Expected:** High near ink 60 and 136, where most images are; near 0 above 230: almost no digit has that much ink.

**Build 2, ask:** Why is the 1s’ hump taller and narrower?

**Expected:** The 1s all use about the same ink, so their hump is narrow; and they are a little more common: 53%.

**Build 3, ask:** Can you get the top picture back from the bottom curve alone?

**Expected:** No. p(y | x) says nothing about where the data are, so it cannot score or draw an x. The top gives the bottom, never the other way.

**Point here:** This is Bayes’ rule, as in Lecture 1, with the label y as the unknown: p(y | x) = p(x | y) p(y) / p(x). Humps: Gaussian kernel densities (bandwidths 6.2 and 3.1) of the 5,923 training 0s and 6,742 1s.

## ae-overfit: Overfitting: too few digits, and the codes fly apart

**Build 1, ask:** Why does the training error keep falling while the test error rises?

**Expected:** The network memorises its 200 digits: it pushes their codes apart, so each gets a private spot the decoder can draw exactly. New digits were never part of that.

**Build 2, ask:** Why does our 3 come back as a 2?

**Expected:** The decoder only learned to draw its 200 training digits. Our 3’s code lands near a training 2’s, so the rebuild is closer to that 2 (error 33.7) than to our 3 (65.6).

**Point here:** Every step uses all 200 digits (no batches), Adam with step size 0.001, on the CPU. “Zoom to fit” rescales each epoch, so you can see the shape; “same axes” shows the drift.

## ae-empirical: Why not sample from the real codes instead of N(0, I)?

**Build 1, ask:** What do we get if we decode a real code exactly?

**Expected:** The training digit it came from, blurred by the 2-number code: a copy, not a new digit.

**Build 2, ask:** Where can the jittered codes go wrong?

**Expected:** Between two clusters: the decoder never trained on codes there, so it blends two digits.

**Build 3, ask:** Why does this beat N(0, I) for the plain autoencoder?

**Expected:** It follows where the real codes are, so every digit type is covered, not just the corner N(0, I) reaches.

**Build 4, ask:** What does the VAE give that this two-step recipe does not?

**Expected:** One model and one objective: codes that fill N(0, I) by design, and a lower bound on log p(x) to score an image.

**Point here:** “In a gap”: the nearest training code is further than 0.26, the 99th percentile of that distance for the 10,000 test digits. Copies: 0%; jitter: 2.3%; 10 Gaussians: 1.4%; N(0, I): 0.7% (10,000 draws each). With 60,000 codes in 2 numbers the plane is crowded; the real cost of N(0, I) is the digits it misses, not gaps.

**Transition:** What we want instead.

**Source / evidence:** Lecture 2’s 2-number autoencoder and its 10-Gaussian fit to the training codes; draws and decodes: <code>lecture5/scripts/empirical.py</code>.

## fix-prior: Fix 1: choose where the codes live, before training

**Build 1, ask:** Why not let the network choose the code distribution itself?

**Expected:** Then we would not know how to sample from it: that was the autoencoder’s problem.

**Build 2, ask:** Why N(0, I) and not, say, a uniform square?

**Expected:** Any simple, known distribution would do. N(0, I) is smooth, has no edges, and its KL has a formula (Fix 3).

**Build 3, ask:** What must training now achieve?

**Expected:** The codes of real images must spread over N(0, I), with no large holes.

**Point here:** In Part 5 this choice becomes the prior p(z), and it keeps the green colour of Lecture 1’s prior.

**Transition:** Fix 2: fill the gaps.

**Source / evidence:** Networks and numbers from Lecture 2: <code>lecture2/scripts/train.py</code> and <code>science.py</code> (MNIST, fixed seeds).

## fix-cloud: Fix 2: send each image to a small cloud of codes, not a point

**Build 1, ask:** Which parts of the code plane does an autoencoder’s decoder see in training?

**Expected:** Only the exact codes of training images.

**Build 2, ask:** What does the decoder learn when clouds overlap?

**Expected:** To draw something sensible everywhere the clouds reach, including between images.

**Point here:** A sketch, not data. Lecture 2, Section 6.2 calls Part 4 “Road 1” to the VAE.

**Transition:** How a network outputs a cloud.

## fix-net: How a network outputs a cloud: a mean, a spread and a random draw

**Build 1, ask:** Why output log σ² instead of σ?

**Expected:** Any real number is a valid log variance; σ = exp(½ log σ²) is then positive.

**Build 2, ask:** Why reparameterise instead of sampling z directly?

**Expected:** sample() blocks the gradient path from the loss to μ and σ.

**Build 3, ask:** At test time, which code do we use to rebuild?

**Expected:** Usually the mean μ.

**Transition:** Fix 3: what stops the clouds drifting apart?

## fix-kl: Fix 3: a penalty that pulls every cloud towards N(0, I)

**Build 1, ask:** What would the encoder do without this penalty?

**Expected:** Make clouds tiny and far apart: reconstruction is easiest that way.

**Build 2, ask:** Why a KL and not just μ²?

**Expected:** μ² would pull clouds to the centre but let σ shrink to 0. The KL stops both, and Part 5 shows it is exactly what Lecture 1’s ELBO asks for.

**Build 3, ask:** Which term punishes a shrinking cloud?

**Expected:** −log σ²: it grows without limit as σ → 0.

**Transition:** Put the three fixes together.

**Source / evidence:** Networks and numbers from Lecture 2: <code>lecture2/scripts/train.py</code> and <code>science.py</code> (MNIST, fixed seeds).

## fix-loss: The three fixes give a loss

**Build 1, ask:** Which term is the autoencoder’s loss?

**Expected:** The first: squared error between the image and its rebuild.

**Build 2, ask:** What would a weight of 0 on the KL give?

**Expected:** An autoencoder with noisy codes; in practice the clouds shrink to points (Section 5.8, β = 0).

**Build 3, ask:** What would a probability model add?

**Expected:** A reason for the loss and its weights, and a score for images: a lower bound on log p(x).

**Transition:** The two terms pull against each other.

## vae-tug: Two terms pull in opposite directions

**Build 1, ask:** What does the reconstruction term want the clouds to do?

**Expected:** Shrink (less noise in z) and spread apart (no confusion between images).

**Build 2, ask:** What does the KL term want?

**Expected:** Move to the centre and widen to σ = 1.

**Build 3, ask:** Where does the balance land?

**Expected:** Clouds near the centre, small but not tiny, slightly overlapping.

**Build 4, ask:** Why does sampling now work?

**Expected:** The codes fill the region N(0, I) samples from, and the decoder was trained across it.

**Point here:** A sketch, not data. Section 5.6 is the real version for our 3, and Section 5.8 shows β = 0 (panel one) and β = 256 (panel two).

## part-5: Part 5 · Why this loss: Lecture 1’s ELBO, one image at a time

## same-problem: Same problem as Lecture 1: the same picture, new names

**Build 1, ask:** Why could Lecture 1 not compute the posterior?

**Expected:** The evidence p(𝒟): an integral over all seven weights, with no formula.

**Build 2, ask:** What plays the role of θ today, and what plays 𝒟?

**Expected:** The code z is the unknown with a prior (θ’s role); the image x is what we observe (𝒟’s role).

**Build 3, ask:** Why not fit 60,000 separate q’s, as Lecture 1 fitted one?

**Expected:** It would take an optimisation per image, and again for every new image. One pass of the encoder is instant: amortised inference.

**Point here:** Careful with θ: in Lecture 1 it was the unknown; today it names the decoder’s weights, learned as ordinary parameters. The unknown with a prior is z. Colours as in Lecture 1: prior green, likelihood orange, evidence blue, posterior teal, q purple, KL to the posterior red.

**Transition:** Start with the model’s story, from z.
