A tempting answer

The average is a terrible sample

The mean is a number that almost never occurs. Generating needs the whole spread of the data, not one summary of it.

This is why "train a network with squared error to output an image" gives blurry averages.

Our first model: one Gaussian · how it scores a number

How probable is $x = 1.5$ under a bump at 2?

$$\N(x;\mu,\sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big)$$
$$\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma} = \frac{1.5 - 2}{0.5} = -1$$
$$\exp\!\big(-\tfrac12\cdot(-1)^2\big) = e^{-0.5} = 0.607$$
$$\frac{1}{0.5\sqrt{2\pi}} = 0.798 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad 0.798\times0.607 = \mathbf{ 0.484 }$$

The model's two parameters, $\mu$ and $\sigma$, decide how high the curve is at every $x$. Higher means more probable.

The formula has two parts: how many standard deviations x is from the centre (squared, inside the exponent), and a constant that makes the total area 1.

A density is a height, not a probability: it can exceed 1 for a narrow bump. Only areas are probabilities. For comparing parameter choices, heights are what we need.

Step 1 · Learn

Score a choice of $\mu, \sigma$ on all 200 numbers

$$\log p_\theta(\text{data}) = \sum_{i=1}^{200} \log \N(x_i;\mu,\sigma^2), \qquad \theta = (\mu, \sigma)$$
$\log p = −3746.0$
$\log p = −1790.0$
$\log p = −430.0$

Learning = keep the $\theta$ with the highest score. That is maximum likelihood, the same rule that trained the name model.

Why the log? The likelihood is a product of 200 heights, a tiny number. The log turns it into a sum, and the best θ is the same.

Left: a narrow bump on the right clump. It fits those points perfectly, but every point on the left clump is 8 standard deviations away, so the score collapses. Middle: a narrow bump in the gap misses every point by about 4 standard deviations, which costs less in total. Right: the best single Gaussian, wide enough to cover both clumps.

Step 1 · Learn, then Step 2 · Generate

The best Gaussian has a formula; then we sample from it

$$\hat\mu = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i = −0.22 \qquad \hat\sigma^2 = \frac1N \sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i-\hat\mu)^2 = 4.31$$
$$x_{\text{new}} = \hat\mu + \hat\sigma\,\eps,\quad \eps \sim \N(0,1)$$

Setting the derivative of the score to zero gives these two numbers: the same answer as the third candidate.

Step 2: 11 of 30 samples land in the empty gap. Learning worked; the model was too simple.

A richer model: tell a story

Hidden choice first, then the number

This is how we will generate: run the two steps. The coin is never written down in the data, so it is a hidden (latent) variable $z$.

Read the recipe aloud: flip a coin, pick that bump, draw a number from it. Two simple steps make two clumps, which one Gaussian could not.

How the new model scores a number

To score $x$, add up every way it could have been made

$$p(x) = \underbrace{\tfrac12\,\N(x;-2,0.5^2)}_{\text{made by bump A}} + \underbrace{\tfrac12\,\N(x;\,2,0.5^2)}_{\text{made by bump B}} = \sum_{z} \cz{p(z)}\,\cdec{p(x\mid z)}$$
$$\text{at } x=1.5:\quad \tfrac12\cdot 0.000 + \tfrac12\cdot 0.484 = 0.242$$

One hidden coin flip made the model rich enough: the samples now fall in the bumps, not in the gap.

Read the formula aloud: probability of x through bump A plus probability of x through bump B, each weighted by how likely that bump is. Summing over the hidden choice is called marginalising it out.

The A-bump contributes about 2 × 10⁻¹² at x = 1.5: 7 standard deviations away.

Step 1 · Learn, for the two-bump model

To fit two bumps, guess which bump made each point

If we knew the coin for every point

fitting is easy: average the A-points for bump A's centre, the B-points for bump B's.

We don't, so ask for each point

given this $x$, how probable is it that bump B made it? That is $p(\mathrm{B}\mid x)$.

Running the story backwards, from a number to its likely hidden cause, is called inference. It is part of learning, not a detour.

Build 3: each of the 200 points coloured by p(B | x), computed on the next slide. Points in the middle are grey: unsure.

For the curious: alternating "guess the bump" and "refit the bumps" is the EM algorithm. We will not need its details; the point is that learning a hidden-variable model needs this backwards step.

What is a "random code"?

$z \sim \N(0, I)$: two independent bell curves

Draw $z_1$ from a standard bell curve $\N(0, 1)$: mean 0, spread 1. Draw $z_2$ the same way, independently.

Together that is written $\N(0, I)$: the 0 says the centre is $(0, 0)$; the I (identity matrix) says each number has spread 1 and the two do not depend on each other.

Why this choice? It is the simplest distribution we can sample from. The decoder, though, was never told about it.

What we want instead

Encode to a cloud, not a point

Known: pull every cloud towards $\N(0, I)$, so random draws land on real codes.
Smooth: every point in a cloud must decode to that image, so neighbourhoods are meaningful.

If the encoder outputs a small region instead of a point, and the decoder must reconstruct x from any point in that region, then the space between nearby codes gets trained. Pulling all regions towards 𝒩(0, I) makes the space compact and tells us where to sample.

That is the variational autoencoder in one picture. The rest of the theory explains why this is the principled loss, not just a trick.

KL, step 3 of 3

Add them up: that is the KL divergence

$\KL = 0$ only when $Q = P$ exactly.

KL is the average extra surprise you pay for believing $Q$ when the truth is $P$.

Why can't the total be negative? To over-predict 7s, Q had to under-predict 1s, and 1s carry more weight. Gibbs' inequality makes this precise.

Start from what we want

Deriving the ELBO · move 2 of 7

Multiply and divide by the encoder's cloud

$$\log \int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz$$
$$= \log \int \hl{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\;\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{\hl{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}}\,dz$$
Multiplying and dividing by the same positive number changes nothing. We are free to choose any $q$; we choose the encoder's Gaussian, which is positive everywhere.

Why bother? Because the next move turns this integral into an average under $q$, and $q$ knows where the patch is.

Deriving the ELBO · move 3 of 7

Read the integral as an average under $q$

$$\log \int q_\varphi(z\mid x)\,\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\,dz$$

From the $\E$ slide: $\int q(z)\,f(z)\,dz = \E_{z\sim q}[f(z)]$. Here $f(z)$ is the ratio $p_\theta(x,z)/q_\varphi(z\mid x)$: one number for each code.

Deriving the ELBO · move 4 of 7

Swap the log and the average: the only inequality

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \log\,\E_{q}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\right]$$
$$\log p_\theta(x) \;\hl{\ge}\; \E_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\right] \;=:\; \ELBO$$
Jensen with $R = p_\theta(x,z)/q_\varphi(z\mid x)$. The right side is the Evidence Lower BOund: it can never exceed $\log p_\theta(x)$.

The log is now inside the average. That is what makes the next three moves possible.

Deriving the ELBO · move 5 of 7

Split the log of a product

$$\ELBO = \E_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(x\mid z)\,p(z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\right]$$
$$= \E_{q}\Big[\hl{\log p_\theta(x\mid z) + \log p(z) - \log q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\Big]$$
First write $p_\theta(x,z) = p_\theta(x\mid z)\,p(z)$: decoder times prior. Then $\log\frac{ab}{c} = \log a + \log b - \log c$.

Deriving the ELBO · move 6 of 7

The average of a sum is the sum of the averages

$$\E_{q}\Big[\log p_\theta(x\mid z) + \log p(z) - \log q_\varphi(z\mid x)\Big]$$
$$= \hl{\E_{q}\big[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)\big]} \;+\; \hl{\E_{q}\big[\log p(z) - \log q_\varphi(z\mid x)\big]}$$
$\E[a + b] = \E[a] + \E[b]$. Group the decoder term on its own, and the two terms that only involve $z$ together.

Deriving the ELBO · move 7 of 7

Recognise the second average: it is a KL

$$\E_{q}\big[\log p(z) - \log q_\varphi(z\mid x)\big]$$
$$= -\,\E_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}{p(z)}\right] = \hl{-\KL\big(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}$$
Flip the sign to flip the fraction. Then use the definition: $\KL(q\|p) = \E_{z\sim q}\big[\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z)}\big]$, the average extra surprise.

And for Gaussians we already have its formula: $\tfrac12\sum_j(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-1-\log\sigma_j^2)$.

Put the pieces together

The ELBO, in symbols and in words

$$\ELBO = \underbrace{\E_{z\sim\cenc q}\big[\cx{\log p_\theta(x\mid z)}\big]}_{\text{rebuild } x \text{ from codes in its cloud}} \;-\; \underbrace{\closs{\KL\big(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}}_{\text{keep the cloud near } \N(0,I)}$$
First term: the autoencoder's reconstruction score, with the code drawn from the cloud.
Second term: the Gaussian KL we just derived.

The two wishes from the autoencoder slide, derived rather than invented. The loss we minimise is $-\ELBO$.

The problem

A sampling step sits in the middle of the graph

In PyTorch, Normal(mu, sigma).sample() returns a tensor with no gradient history: the graph is cut at z.

We want the gradient of the expected loss with respect to μ and σ: if the cloud moves a little, how does the average loss change? There are other estimators (score function / REINFORCE), but they are noisy. Here is a simple trick that works for Gaussians.

The trick

Draw standard noise first, then stretch and shift it

$$z = \mu + \sigma\,\eps,\quad \eps\sim\N(0,1) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad z \sim \N(\mu,\sigma^2)$$

Same distribution as before. But the randomness now enters as an input, $\eps$, that does not depend on any weights.

Follow one dot (red): ε = −0.4, scaled by σ = 0.5 to −0.2, shifted by μ = 1 to 0.8. Each z is a deterministic function of (μ, σ, ε).

If μ increases a little, every dot moves right by the same amount. If σ increases, each dot moves away from the centre in proportion to its ε. That is exactly the derivative.

The fix

Now the path from the loss to $\mu$ and $\sigma$ is ordinary arithmetic

Formally: ∇ E_{z∼q}[f(z)] = E_{ε∼𝒩(0,1)}[∇ f(μ + σε)], under mild regularity conditions. We check this numerically two slides from now.

Chain rule, symbol by symbol

Take $f(z) = z^2$ as the loss

$$z = \mu + \sigma\eps \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial \mu} = 1, \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial \sigma} = \eps, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} = 2z$$
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial \mu} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\cdot\frac{\partial z}{\partial \mu} = 2z\cdot 1$$
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial \sigma} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\cdot\frac{\partial z}{\partial \sigma} = 2z\cdot\eps$$

The noise value $\eps$ appears in the $\sigma$-gradient: a draw far from the centre pushes $\sigma$ harder.

ε is treated exactly like an input pixel: a fixed number for this forward and backward pass. A new ε is drawn next step.

Now plug in numbers

$\mu = 1,\ \sigma = 0.5,\ \eps = -0.4$

Finite-difference check with ε held fixed: μ → 1.001 gives z = 0.801 and f = 0.641601, slope 1.601 ≈ 1.6. If you resample ε between the two evaluations, the check becomes noise.

One more link in the chain

The network outputs $v = \log\sigma^2$, not $\sigma$

$\sigma$$=$$e^{v/2}$ $v$ can be any real number; $\sigma$ is always positive $\dfrac{\partial\sigma}{\partial v}$$=$$\tfrac12 e^{v/2} = \dfrac{\sigma}{2} = 0.25$ derivative of $e^{v/2}$ $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial v}$$=$$\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial \sigma}\cdot\dfrac{\partial\sigma}{\partial v} = -0.64 \times 0.25 = -0.16$ one more chain-rule factor

Autograd does exactly this. The point of doing it by hand once: no step is magic.

Is one draw enough?

One draw is noisy; the average is exactly right

$$\E_\eps\big[f\big] = \E\big[(\mu+\sigma\eps)^2\big] = \mu^2 + \sigma^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\E[f] = 2\mu = 2, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}\E[f] = 2\sigma = 1$$

Our single $\eps$ gave 1.6 and $-0.64$. Averaged over 10000 draws: 2.01 and 1.02. SGD only needs the right answer on average.

Each histogram: the per-sample gradient for 10,000 values of ε. The red dashed line is our ε = −0.4. The solid line, the average, sits on the exact gradient of the expected loss. Noisy but unbiased: exactly what stochastic gradient descent needs.

detach() keeps the numbers in fake but forgets how they were computed, so backward() stops there. Without it, G would accumulate gradients from D's loss: wasted work at best, a bug at worst.

Where G's gradient comes from

The chain rule runs through D

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal L_G}{\partial \theta} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial \mathcal L_G}{\partial D}}_{=\,-1/D} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial D}{\partial x}}_{\text{slope of } D \text{ at the fake}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta}}_{\text{how } \theta \text{ moves the fake}}, \qquad x = G_\theta(z)$$
$-1/D$ is always negative: raising $D$ always lowers $\mathcal L_G$.
$\partial D/\partial x$: which way is "more real" at this fake. Needs $D$'s weights, but does not change them.
$\partial x/\partial\theta$: ordinary backprop through $G$.

So the fake moves uphill on $D$, and $G$'s weights change to make that move. Next: watch it on the 1-D data.

This is why the G step must not detach the fake: the middle factor needs the path through D. It is also why D's weights can stay frozen: we need the slope of D, not an update to it.

Freezing is not detaching

Detach the fake in the G step and loss_G still prints a number, but G never learns. Silent failure.

The textbook formula

A two-player minimax game

$$\min_{\cdec G}\;\max_{\cdisc D}\;\; \E_{x\sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\log D(x)\big] \;+\; \E_{z\sim p(z)}\big[\log\big(1 - D(G(z))\big)\big]$$
D maximises: high D on real data, low D on fakes. This is our $-\mathcal L_D$.
G minimises: only the second term involves G. It wants $D(G(z))$ high.

With a perfect D, G's best move is $p_G = p_{\text{data}}$ (Goodfellow et al. 2014). Real training, with finite networks and steps, has no such guarantee.

The optimal discriminator and its link to Jensen-Shannon divergence are in the appendix. Do not claim that GAN training provably converges in practice.

Two generator losses, two slopes

Differentiate both with respect to D's logit $a$

$$D = \mathrm{sigmoid}(a), \qquad \frac{dD}{da} = D\,(1-D)$$

minimax: minimise $\log(1-D)$

$$\frac{d}{da}\log(1-D) = \frac{-D(1-D)}{1-D} = -D$$
$$D = 0.01:\quad -0.01$$

non-saturating: minimise $-\log D$

$$\frac{d}{da}\big(-\log D\big) = \frac{-D(1-D)}{D} = D - 1$$
$$D = 0.01:\quad -0.99$$

Early in training $D$ easily rejects fakes, so $D \approx 0$: the minimax slope vanishes, the non-saturating one does not.

Both losses push D(G(z)) up and share the same ideal solution. They are not equal as functions; they differ in how hard they push when the generator is bad, which is exactly when it needs a push.

Why the code uses a different generator loss

Early on, the minimax loss gives G almost no signal

When D easily spots a fake ($D = 0.01$): slope $-0.01$ for $\log(1-D)$, slope $-0.99$ for $-\log D$. Same goal, a 99× stronger push.

Derivatives with respect to D's logit a, where D = sigmoid(a): d log(1 − sigmoid a)/da = −D; d[−log sigmoid a]/da = D − 1. The two generator losses are not equal; they share the same fixed point but behave very differently early in training.

Appendix C

The optimal discriminator

For fixed G, maximise $\int p_{\text{data}}(x)\log D(x) + p_G(x)\log(1-D(x))\,dx$ pointwise in $D(x)$:

$$D^*(x) = \dfrac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_G(x)}$$

Substituting back:

$$V(D^*, G) = -\log 4 + 2\,\mathrm{JS}(p_{\text{data}}\,\|\,p_G)$$

So with a perfect D, G minimises the Jensen–Shannon divergence, which is 0 only when $p_G = p_{\text{data}}$, where $D^* = \tfrac12$ everywhere. Idealised: real D and G are finite networks trained for finitely many steps.
