What all of these share

Randomness in, a learned network, a sample out

Training means: adjust $\theta$ until the samples look like the data. But what is the loss?

Build 1: the random input. Build 2: a sample. Build 3: an optional condition (a sketch, a class label, a sentence).

The difficulty: there is no target image for a random z. Today we see two answers: the VAE (an encoder tells us which z belongs to which image) and the GAN (a second network learns to judge).

Part 1

What does it mean to learn a distribution?

Before images, one number. Everything we need (fitting, sampling, hidden causes, inference) fits on a number line.

Our 1-D toy data

200 numbers in two clumps

Pretend each number is an image squashed to one value. How would you produce a 201st that looks like it belongs?

Data: 200 draws, half from 𝒩(−2, 0.5²) and half from 𝒩(2, 0.5²). Students do not need to know that yet; they should see two clumps.

Typical answers: "pick a random existing point" (memorising, no new data), "use the average" (next slide), "fit a distribution and sample" (yes).

A tempting answer

The average is a terrible sample

A model that predicts the mean always outputs something that never occurs. Regression thinking does not give generation.

This is why "train a network with MSE to output an image" gives blurry averages. We will see a softer version of this later when VAE samples look blurry.

Fit, then sample

The best single Gaussian, by maximum likelihood

$$\hat\mu = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i = −0.22 \qquad \hat\sigma^2 = \frac1N \sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i-\hat\mu)^2 = 4.31$$
$$x_{\text{new}} = \hat\mu + \hat\sigma\,\eps,\quad \eps \sim \N(0,1)$$

These two numbers make the observed data as probable as any Gaussian can.

11 of 30 samples land in the empty gap. Best fit within the family, wrong shape.

The maximum-likelihood Gaussian has the sample mean and the 1/N variance. Students derived this in the probability lectures.

Lesson: optimisation worked perfectly; the model family was too simple. Better optimisation cannot fix that.

A richer model: tell a story

Hidden choice first, then the number

The coin is never written down in the data. We only ever see $x$. The coin $z$ is a hidden (latent) variable.

Read the sampling recipe aloud: flip a coin, pick that bump, draw a point from it. Two simple steps make a two-bump distribution that one Gaussian could not.

This "generative story" pattern is exactly the VAE: first draw a hidden code z, then draw the image x given z.

One ingredient, worked by hand

How high is bump B at $x = 1.5$?

$$\N(x;\mu,\sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big)$$
$$\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma} = \frac{1.5 - 2}{0.5} = -1$$
$$\exp\!\big(-\tfrac12\cdot(-1)^2\big) = e^{-0.5} = 0.607$$
$$\frac{1}{0.5\sqrt{2\pi}} = 0.798 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad 0.798\times0.607 = \mathbf{ 0.484 }$$

A density is a height, not a probability: it can exceed 1 for a narrow bump. Only areas are probabilities.

The formula has two parts: how many standard deviations away x is (squared, inside the exponent), and a constant that makes the area under the curve 1.

One standard deviation from the centre always gives e^{−1/2} ≈ 0.61 of the peak height.

From the story to a probability

To score $x$, add over every hidden value

$$p(x) = \sum_{z\in\{\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B}\}} \cz{p(z)}\,\cdec{p(x\mid z)} = \tfrac12\,\N(x;-2,0.5^2) + \tfrac12\,\N(x;\,2,0.5^2)$$
$$\text{at } x=1.5:\quad \tfrac12\cdot 0.000 + \tfrac12\cdot 0.484 = 0.242$$

Samples now fall in the bumps. Remember this sum. With images it becomes an integral we cannot compute.

The A-bump contributes about 2 × 10⁻¹² at x = 1.5: 7 standard deviations away.

"Marginalising out z": the probability of what we observe is an average over everything we cannot observe.

Run the story backwards

You see $x = 0.1$. Which bump made it?

bump $z$prior $p(z)$likelihood $p(x\mid z)$prior × likelihood÷ total = posterior

Bayes' rule is just this table: multiply, then divide by the total. $x = 0.1$ is close to the middle, so we stay unsure.

Build by build: the two likelihoods (both tiny: 0.1 is far from both centres, but closer to B), multiply by the prior, add to get the evidence, divide.

The tiny numbers do not matter: only their ratio does. This will matter for images, where likelihoods are like 10⁻⁵⁰.

Do it for every $x$

The posterior, as a curve

Repeat the table for every $x$ and plot $p(\mathrm{B}\mid x)$.

Far right: certainly B. Far left: certainly A. Near 0: unsure.

For these two bumps the curve is exactly $\mathrm{sigmoid}(16x)$: a logistic regression in disguise.

Going from an observation back to its hidden cause is called inference. The VAE's encoder will learn to do it.

Derivation of the sigmoid if asked: the log ratio of the two likelihoods is [(x+2)² − (x−2)²]/(2·0.25) = 16x, and the equal priors cancel.

Here inference is exact because we can compute p(x). For images, p(x) is the hard part, so the posterior will be hard too.

Four words we will use all lecture

Prior, likelihood, evidence, posterior

namesymbolquestion it answersat $x = 0.1$

Keep these four colours in mind: amber for z and the prior, teal for the decoder/likelihood, blue for observed data, purple for the encoder/posterior.

The same story for images

The code is 2 real numbers instead of a coin, and a network maps it to 784 pixel probabilities. Nobody tells it what $z$ means.

Real outputs of the decoder we train later. Step 2 gives a probability per pixel (a grey image). Step 3 flips a coin per pixel. Most papers show step 2 as "the sample" because it looks cleaner.

What actually comes out

Random codes give scribbles

ten codes drawn from $\N(0, I)$ in 16 dimensions, through the trained decoder

The decoder only ever saw codes made by the encoder. Where do those codes live?

Nothing in the training loss mentioned 𝒩(0, I). The encoder was free to put codes anywhere: shifted, stretched, with large gaps. A random code lands somewhere the decoder never trained on.

Look inside (2-number version)

Overlay random codes

Random draws all land in one small spot

150 draws from $\N(0, I)$ in amber: all inside one tiny crowded patch.

ten of them decoded: some digits, some smudges

And we can never generate a 1: the 1s live near $z_1 \approx 20$.

To sample, we need to know where the codes are, and every point there must decode to something sensible.

Two separate failures. (1) Coverage: the region where we sample does not match where codes live, so whole classes are missing. (2) Holes: between clusters the decoder was never trained, so it produces smudges.

What we want instead

Encode to a cloud, not a point

Known: pull every cloud towards $\N(0, I)$, so random draws land on real codes.
Smooth: every point in a cloud must decode to that image, so neighbourhoods are meaningful.

If the encoder outputs a small region instead of a point, and the decoder must reconstruct x from any point in that region, then the space between nearby codes gets trained. Pulling all regions towards 𝒩(0, I) makes the space compact and tells us where to sample.

That is the variational autoencoder in one picture. The rest of the theory explains why this is the principled loss, not just a trick.

A dataset of 1s and 7s, 80% 1s. The model believes 60/40. How wrong is it, as one number?

Keep the story concrete: draw digits from the data, ask the model how probable each was. When a 1 shows up, the model said 0.6 when it should have said 0.8.

Ingredient: surprise

Surprise $= -\log(\text{probability})$

outcome$P$$Q$surprise if you believe $P$surprise if you believe $Q$
a 10.80.6$-\log 0.8 = 0.223$$-\log 0.6 = 0.511$

Certain events cost 0. Rare events cost a lot. Natural logs, so the unit is the nat.
Believing $Q$, a 1 surprises us more than it should ($0.511$ vs $0.223$), a 7 less ($0.916$ vs $1.609$).

This is the same −log p that appeared in the name model and in cross-entropy. Nothing new, just a name for it.

KL, step 1 of 3

For each outcome: how much extra surprise does $Q$ cause?

$$\underbrace{-\log Q_i}_{\text{surprise under } Q} \;-\; \underbrace{(-\log P_i)}_{\text{surprise under } P} \;=\; \log\frac{P_i}{Q_i}$$
outcome$-\log Q_i$$-\log P_i$extra surprise $\log(P_i/Q_i)$
a 10.5110.223$0.511 - 0.223 = 0.288$
a 70.9161.609$0.916 - 1.609 = -0.693$

Negative for the 7: $Q$ over-predicts 7s, so a 7 surprises it less than it should.

KL, step 2 of 3

Weight each outcome by how often it really happens

outcomehow often: $P_i$extra surpriseweighted: $P_i\log(P_i/Q_i)$
a 10.80.288$0.8 \times 0.288 = 0.230$
a 70.2$-0.693$$0.2 \times (-0.693) = -0.139$

Real data is 80% 1s, so the 1 row counts four times as much as the 7 row.

Why weight by P and not by Q? Because P is what actually happens. We ask: on the data we really see, how much extra surprise does the model cause per example?

KL, step 3 of 3

Add them up: that is the KL divergence

$$\KL(P\,\|\,Q) \;=\; \sum_i P_i \log\frac{P_i}{Q_i}$$
$$= 0.230 + (-0.139) = 0.092 \text{ nats}$$
One row was negative, yet the total can never be: $\KL \ge 0$.
$\KL = 0$ only when $Q = P$ exactly.

KL is the average extra surprise you pay for believing $Q$ when the truth is $P$.

Why can't the total be negative? To over-predict 7s, Q had to under-predict 1s, and 1s carry more weight. Gibbs' inequality makes this precise.

Notation we will use all lecture

$\E$ means "average over"

$$\E_{x\sim P}\big[f(x)\big] = \sum_x P(x)\,f(x)$$
exact, for a sum over outcomes
$$\E_{z\sim q}\big[f(z)\big] = \int q(z)\,f(z)\,dz$$
exact, for a continuous variable
$$\E_{x\sim P}\big[f(x)\big] \approx \frac1S\sum_{s=1}^{S} f(x_s),\quad x_s\sim P$$
estimated from samples
KL is an average: $\KL(P\|Q) = \E_{x\sim P}\big[\log\tfrac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\big]$. Five draws 1, 1, 7, 1, 1 give $\tfrac15(4\times 0.288 - 0.693) = 0.092$.

The subscript says which distribution the average is taken under: x ∼ P means "x is drawn from P". This matters because KL changes when you swap it.

Those five draws happen to be exactly 80/20, so the estimate matches. With random draws it would fluctuate around 0.092.

Not a distance

Swap the arguments, get a different number

$$\KL(Q\|P) = 0.6\log\frac{0.6}{0.8} + 0.4\log\frac{0.4}{0.2}$$
$$= -0.173 + 0.277 = 0.105$$

$0.105 \neq 0.092$: KL is not symmetric. "How wrong is $Q$ about $P$" differs from "how wrong is $P$ about $Q$".

Push $q \to 0$ and $\KL(P\|Q) \to \infty$. Never give zero probability to something that happens.

model's $q$ =0.60 $\KL(P\|Q)$ = 0.092$\KL(Q\|P)$ = 0.105

Drag the slider live. Both curves are zero at q = 0.8. Near q = 0 the red curve explodes: the data has 1s but the model says they never happen.

You have minimised a KL many times

Cross-entropy = entropy + KL

$H(P,Q)$$=$$\displaystyle -\sum_i P_i\log Q_i$ cross-entropy: the loss you train classifiers with $=$$\displaystyle -\sum_i P_i\log P_i \;+\; \sum_i P_i\big(\log P_i - \log Q_i\big)$ add and subtract $\sum_i P_i\log P_i$ $=$$\displaystyle \underbrace{H(P)}_{\text{entropy: fixed by the data}} \;+\; \KL(P\,\|\,Q)$

Lowering cross-entropy can only lower the KL part. So does maximum likelihood: it minimises $\KL(\text{data}\,\|\,\text{model})$.

Together: H(P, Q) = 0.592 nats. Remember the direction: data first. That direction will explain why VAEs blur.

The version the VAE needs

KL between two bumps: an area

For continuous variables the sum becomes an integral:

$$\KL(q\|p) = \int q(z)\,\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z)}\,dz$$

Take $q = \N(1, 0.5^2)$, the encoder's cloud, and $p = \N(0,1)$, the prior.

The shaded area, computed numerically, is 0.818 nats.

Can we get 0.818 without a computer? Yes: for two Gaussians the integral has a formula. Three steps.

Numerical integral of the lower curve: 0.8181. The grey part is where q is below p and contributes negatively, like the 7 row earlier.

Gaussian KL, step 1 of 2

Write out the log of the ratio

$\log q(z)$$=$$\displaystyle -\log\sigma - \tfrac12\log 2\pi - \frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$ log of $\N(z;\mu,\sigma^2)$ $\log p(z)$$=$$\displaystyle -\tfrac12\log 2\pi - \frac{z^2}{2}$ log of $\N(z;0,1)$ $\log\dfrac{q(z)}{p(z)}$$=$$\displaystyle -\log\sigma - \frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{z^2}{2}$ subtract: the $\tfrac12\log 2\pi$ terms cancel

Three terms. Next: average each one over $z \sim q$.

Gaussian KL, step 2 of 2

Average each term over the cloud $q$

$\E_q\big[(z-\mu)^2\big] = \sigma^2$ (that is the variance)
$\E_q\big[z^2\big] = \mu^2 + \sigma^2$ (mean squared + variance)
$$\KL = -\log\sigma - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{\mu^2+\sigma^2}{2} = \frac12\Big(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2\Big)$$
$$\mu = 1,\ \sigma = 0.5:\quad \tfrac12\big(1 + 0.25 - 1 + 1.386\big) = 0.818 \ \checkmark$$

Moving the mean away costs $\mu^2$; shrinking $\sigma$ costs $-\log\sigma^2$. For a code with several coordinates, add one such term per coordinate.

The check matches the shaded area from two slides ago. This exact formula is what the VAE code computes.

Back to our two bumps

Fit one Gaussian: the KL direction decides what you get

minimise $\KL(\text{data}\,\|\,q)$: cover everything.
This is the maximum-likelihood fit from before.

minimise $\KL(q\,\|\,\text{data})$: never put mass where
data is absent, so pick one bump.

Mode-covering vs mode-seeking. Keep this picture: it explains blurry VAEs and mode-collapsing GANs.

Both fits found by brute-force search over (μ, σ). Forward: 𝒩(0.0, 2.06²), the same as the moment-matched maximum-likelihood fit. Reverse: 𝒩(−2.0, 0.5²), sitting on one bump.

Why: KL(p‖q) is weighted by p, so q is punished wherever data exists and q is small. KL(q‖p) is weighted by q, so q is punished wherever it puts mass and the data is absent.

Part 4 · tool 2

The ELBO: a loss we can actually compute

Goal: give our 3 high probability, as with the names. Problem: for a latent-variable model that probability is an integral nobody can evaluate. Watch it happen on our 3.

The model, as a picture

Two arrows: generate down, infer up

$$p_\theta(x, z) = \cz{p(z)}\;\cdec{p_\theta(x\mid z)}$$

Solid arrow: the generative model we want, prior times decoder. Dashed arrow: an approximate inference network we add only to make training possible. θ are decoder weights, φ are encoder weights.

Same story as the coin and the bumps; z is now a 2-D code and p(x|z) is a neural network.

Maximum likelihood for our 3

How probable is our 3 under the model?

With the coin we summed over two values:

$$p(x) = \sum_{z\in\{\mathrm A,\mathrm B\}} p(z)\,p(x\mid z)$$

A code can be any point in the plane, so the sum becomes an integral:

$$p_\theta(x) = \int \cz{p(z)}\;\cdec{p_\theta(x\mid z)}\,dz$$

2 numbers per code, so we can afford a 901 × 901 grid of decoder calls: $\log p_\theta(x) = −128.7$ nats. With 16 numbers it would be $901^{16}$ calls.

The map shows log p(x | z) for our 3 at every code z: dark = codes that decode to something like our 3. Dashed circles: 1, 2, 3 prior standard deviations.

Real computation on the trained 2-D VAE decoder: 811,801 decoder calls, then log-sum-exp.

Idea: estimate the integral by sampling

Random codes almost never hit the right patch

$$p_\theta(x) = \E_{z\sim p(z)}\big[\,p_\theta(x\mid z)\,\big] \approx \frac1S\sum_{s=1}^{S} p_\theta(x\mid z_s)$$
100,000random codes $z_s\sim\N(0,I)$
0.07%land inside our 3's patch
−363.9typical $\log p(x\mid z)$ of a random code
−121.0the best code's $\log p(x\mid z)$

A typical random code makes our 3 about $e^{243}$ times less probable than the best code. Blind sampling wastes almost every draw.

The estimate is unbiased but its variance is enormous: the average is dominated by the rare draws that hit the patch, and with 16 or 100 dimensions you would essentially never hit it.

Better: ask someone where the patch is. That "someone" is the encoder.

Inference, like the coin and the bumps

Ask the encoder where to look

The patch, normalised, is the true posterior:

$$p_\theta(z\mid x) = \frac{p(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)}{p_\theta(x)}$$

It needs $p_\theta(x)$, the impossible integral. So we train a network to guess it:

$$\cenc{q_\varphi(z\mid x)} = \N\big(\mu_\varphi(x),\, \sigma_\varphi(x)^2\big)$$

For our 3: $\mu = (0.3,\ −1.93)$ and $\sigma = (0.047,\ 0.05)$. Close to the truth, not identical.

Zoomed view around our 3. Shaded: the true posterior from the grid. Purple ellipses: one and two standard deviations of the encoder's Gaussian. The true posterior is slightly tilted and offset; a diagonal Gaussian cannot tilt.

Notice the scale: σ ≈ 0.05 while the prior has σ = 1. The encoder is very confident about where our 3 lives.

Deriving the ELBO · move 1 of 7

Start from what we want

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \log \int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz$$
Maximum likelihood wants this number large for our 3, and for every image in the data. The integral over all codes is the part we cannot compute.

We will rewrite this line until it becomes something we can compute and differentiate.

Recall p(x, z) = p(z) p(x | z): prior times decoder. Each of the next six slides makes exactly one change. Ask the class to name the move before you click.

Deriving the ELBO · move 2 of 7

Multiply and divide by the encoder's cloud

$$\log \int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz$$
$$= \log \int \hl{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\;\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{\hl{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}}\,dz$$
Multiplying and dividing by the same positive number changes nothing. We are free to choose any $q$; we choose the encoder's Gaussian, which is positive everywhere.

Why bother? Because the next move turns this integral into an average under $q$, and $q$ knows where the patch is.

Deriving the ELBO · move 3 of 7

Read the integral as an average under $q$

$$\log \int q_\varphi(z\mid x)\,\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\,dz$$
$$= \log\; \hl{\E_{z\sim q_\varphi(z\mid x)}}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\right]$$
From the $\E$ slide: $\int q(z)\,f(z)\,dz = \E_{z\sim q}[f(z)]$. Here $f(z)$ is the ratio $p_\theta(x,z)/q_\varphi(z\mid x)$: one number for each code.

Still exact. We now average over codes drawn from the encoder's cloud, right where the patch is.

Deriving the ELBO · a tool for move 4

Jensen's inequality, in one picture

$\log$ curves downwards (it is concave), so every chord lies below the curve.

Two values, $R=1$ and $R=4$:

$$\underbrace{\log 2.5}_{\log \text{ of the average}} = 0.916 \;\ge\; \underbrace{\tfrac12(0 + 1.386)}_{\text{average of the logs}} = 0.693$$
$$\log \E[R] \;\ge\; \E[\log R]$$

Read the picture: two orange points on the curve; their chord lies below the curve. The midpoint of the chord (average of logs) sits below the curve at the midpoint (log of the average).

Deriving the ELBO · move 4 of 7

Swap the log and the average: the only inequality

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \log\,\E_{q}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\right]$$
$$\log p_\theta(x) \;\hl{\ge}\; \E_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\right] \;=:\; \ELBO$$
Jensen with $R = p_\theta(x,z)/q_\varphi(z\mid x)$. The right side is the Evidence Lower BOund: it can never exceed $\log p_\theta(x)$.

The log is now inside the average. That is what makes the next three moves possible.

Deriving the ELBO · move 5 of 7

Split the log of a product

$$\ELBO = \E_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(x\mid z)\,p(z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\right]$$
$$= \E_{q}\Big[\hl{\log p_\theta(x\mid z) + \log p(z) - \log q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\Big]$$
First write $p_\theta(x,z) = p_\theta(x\mid z)\,p(z)$: decoder times prior. Then $\log\frac{ab}{c} = \log a + \log b - \log c$.

Deriving the ELBO · move 6 of 7

The average of a sum is the sum of the averages

$$\E_{q}\Big[\log p_\theta(x\mid z) + \log p(z) - \log q_\varphi(z\mid x)\Big]$$
$$= \hl{\E_{q}\big[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)\big]} \;+\; \hl{\E_{q}\big[\log p(z) - \log q_\varphi(z\mid x)\big]}$$
$\E[a + b] = \E[a] + \E[b]$. Group the decoder term on its own, and the two terms that only involve $z$ together.

Deriving the ELBO · move 7 of 7

Recognise the second average: it is a KL

$$\E_{q}\big[\log p(z) - \log q_\varphi(z\mid x)\big]$$
$$= -\,\E_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}{p(z)}\right] = \hl{-\KL\big(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}$$
Flip the sign to flip the fraction. Then use the definition: $\KL(q\|p) = \E_{z\sim q}\big[\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z)}\big]$, the average extra surprise.

And for Gaussians we already have its formula: $\tfrac12\sum_j(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-1-\log\sigma_j^2)$.

Put the pieces together

The ELBO, in symbols and in words

$$\ELBO = \underbrace{\E_{z\sim\cenc q}\big[\cx{\log p_\theta(x\mid z)}\big]}_{\text{rebuild } x \text{ from codes in its cloud}} \;-\; \underbrace{\closs{\KL\big(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}}_{\text{keep the cloud near } \N(0,I)}$$
First term: the autoencoder's reconstruction score, with the code drawn from the cloud.
Second term: the Gaussian KL we just derived.

The two wishes from the autoencoder slide, derived rather than invented. The loss we minimise is $-\ELBO$.

What did the inequality cost?

A second route: start from the true posterior

$\KL\big(q\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\big)$$=$$\E_q\big[\log q_\varphi(z\mid x) - \log p_\theta(z\mid x)\big]$ definition of KL, now against the true posterior $=$$\E_q\big[\log q_\varphi(z\mid x) - \log p_\theta(x,z)\big] + \log p_\theta(x)$ Bayes: $\log p_\theta(z\mid x) = \log p_\theta(x,z) - \log p_\theta(x)$, and $\log p_\theta(x)$ has no $z$ in it $=$$-\ELBO + \log p_\theta(x)$ the average is exactly minus the ELBO from move 4
$$\log p_\theta(x) = \ELBO + \KL\big(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\big)$$

The gap is a KL, so it is never negative: a second proof that the ELBO is a lower bound.

The reconstruction term, zoomed in

Each pixel is a coin flip with probability $\pi_i$

our 3, one 5 × 5 patch

$$-\log p_\theta(x\mid z) = -\sum_{i=1}^{784}\Big[x_i\log\pi_i + (1-x_i)\log(1-\pi_i)\Big] = 122.3\ \text{nats}$$

Binary cross-entropy is not a design choice: it is the Bernoulli log-likelihood. Gaussian pixels would give squared error.

The patch is on the top stroke of our 3. Where x = 1 the loss is −log π; where x = 0 it is −log(1 − π). Ink predicted at 0.89 costs 0.11; ink predicted at 0.18 costs 1.71.

The VAE loss for our 3

$$\mathcal L(x) = -\ELBO \approx \underbrace{\closs{-\log p_\theta(x\mid z)}}_{\text{BCE over 784 pixels}} \;+\; \underbrace{\closs{\tfrac12\sum_{j=1}^{2}\big(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-1-\log\sigma_j^2\big)}}_{\text{KL over 2 coordinates}},\qquad z\sim q_\varphi(z\mid x)$$
